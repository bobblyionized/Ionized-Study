<h2>4.5 Rational Functions</h2>
<p>A <b>rational function</b> is a quotient of polynomials, $f(x)=\dfrac{g(x)}{h(x)}$, such as $\dfrac1x$, $\dfrac{4x-3}{2x+1}$, or $\dfrac{2x^3+5x+2}{x^2-7x+6}$. Its domain is every $x$ with $h(x)\ne0$. Unlike polynomials, their graphs can break apart and shoot off to infinity.</p>

<h3>1. The Big-Little Principle</h3>
<div class="box">If $c$ is far from $0$, then $\frac1c$ is close to $0$; if $c$ is close to $0$, then $\frac1c$ is far from $0$:
$$\frac{1}{\text{big}}=\text{little},\qquad \frac{1}{\text{little}}=\text{big}.$$</div>
<p>This explains the two basic graphs. Near $x=0$, $\frac1x$ and $\frac1{x^2}$ explode (the $y$-axis is a <b>vertical asymptote</b>); far from $0$ they shrink toward $0$ (the $x$-axis is a <b>horizontal asymptote</b>). An asymptote is a line the graph gets closer and closer to.</p>
<div class="lfigs">
<div class="lplot" data-f="1/x" data-x="-5,5" data-y="-5,5" data-w="180" data-cap="$y=\frac1x$"></div>
<div class="lplot" data-f="1/(x*x)" data-x="-5,5" data-y="-2,6" data-w="180" data-cap="$y=\frac1{x^2}$"></div>
</div>

<h3>2. Linear rational functions</h3>
<p>For $f(x)=\dfrac{ax+b}{cx+d}$ (with $c\ne0$ and $ad-bc\ne0$):</p>
<div class="box"><ul><li>vertical asymptote $x=-\frac dc$ (where the denominator is $0$);</li>
<li>horizontal asymptote $y=\frac ac$ (the ratio of leading coefficients).</li></ul></div>
<p>Why the horizontal asymptote: divide top and bottom by $x$: $\dfrac{a+\frac bx}{c+\frac dx}$. For big $|x|$, $\frac bx$ and $\frac dx$ are little, so $f(x)\approx\frac ac$. Example: $\dfrac{x+1}{2x-4}$ has asymptotes $x=2$ and $y=\frac12$. Every such graph is a stretched, shifted copy of $\frac1x$.</p>
<div class="lfigs"><div class="lplot" data-f="(x+1)/(2*x-4);0.5" data-x="-6,8" data-y="-5,5" data-w="230" data-cap="$\frac{x+1}{2x-4}$: asymptotes $x=2$, $y=\frac12$"></div></div>

<h3>3. Intercepts, vertical asymptotes, and holes</h3>
<p>Write $f=\frac gh$ in <b>lowest terms</b> first (cancel common factors).</p>
<ul><li><b>$y$-intercept:</b> $f(0)$, if $0$ is in the domain.</li>
<li><b>$x$-intercepts:</b> the zeros of the numerator (in lowest terms).</li>
<li><b>Vertical asymptotes:</b> the zeros of the denominator (in lowest terms).</li>
<li><b>Holes:</b> a factor $x-c$ that cancels leaves a single missing point at $x=c$.</li></ul>
<p>Example: $f(x)=\dfrac{x^2-4}{x^2-x-2}=\dfrac{(x-2)(x+2)}{(x-2)(x+1)}$. Cancel: $\dfrac{x+2}{x+1}$ for $x\ne2$. Vertical asymptote $x=-1$; a <b>hole</b> at $x=2$, at height $\frac{4}{3}$; $x$-intercept $-2$; $y$-intercept $2$.</p>

<h3>4. Horizontal asymptotes in general</h3>
<div class="box">Let the numerator have degree $n$ and leading coefficient $a$, and the denominator degree $k$ and leading coefficient $c$.
<ul><li>$n\lt k$: horizontal asymptote $y=0$.</li>
<li>$n=k$: horizontal asymptote $y=\frac ac$.</li>
<li>$n\gt k$: no horizontal asymptote (Section 4.5.A covers what happens instead).</li></ul></div>
<p>A graph may cross its horizontal asymptote (in the middle); it can never cross a vertical one.</p>

<h3>5. Applications</h3>
<ul><li><b>Average cost:</b> if making $x$ items costs $C(x)=5000+12x$, the average cost per item is $A(x)=\frac{5000+12x}{x}$. Its horizontal asymptote $y=12$ says the average approaches the per-item cost as production grows. For $A(x)\le14$: $5000+12x\le14x$, so $x\ge2500$.</li>
<li><b>Mixtures:</b> adding $x$ liters of pure acid to $10$ liters of $20\%$ acid gives concentration $\frac{2+x}{10+x}$, approaching $1$ (100%). To reach $50\%$: $2+x=5+0.5x$, $x=6$.</li></ul>

<h3>Worked examples</h3>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 1.</b> Find the asymptotes of $y=\dfrac1{x-3}+2$.
<details><summary>Solution</summary>It is $\frac1x$ shifted right $3$ and up $2$: vertical asymptote $x=3$, horizontal asymptote $y=2$. (As one fraction: $\frac{2x-5}{x-3}$, ratio of leading coefficients $2$.)</details></div>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 2.</b> Solve $\dfrac{2x+1}{x-1}=3$.
<details><summary>Solution</summary>$2x+1=3x-3$, so $x=4$ (and $4\ne1$, so it's valid).</details></div>
<div class="warn"><h4>Common traps</h4><ul>
<li>Calling every zero of the original denominator a vertical asymptote. Cancel first: canceled factors are holes.</li>
<li>Using the degree rule backwards: bigger denominator degree gives $y=0$.</li>
<li>Forgetting to exclude values that make the original denominator $0$ when solving equations.</li>
</ul></div>
</script>
