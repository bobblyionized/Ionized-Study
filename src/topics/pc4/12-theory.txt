<h2>4.8 Theory of Equations</h2>
<p>We invented $i$ to solve $x^2+1=0$. Will we need to keep inventing new numbers to solve cubics, quartics, and beyond? The remarkable answer is <b>no</b>: the complex numbers are enough for every polynomial equation.</p>

<h3>1. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Fundamental Theorem of Algebra</h4>Every nonconstant polynomial (even one with complex coefficients) has at least one complex root.</div>
<p>Combined with the Factor Theorem (which works for complex numbers too), this peels off one linear factor at a time:</p>
<div class="box"><h4>Factorization over the complex numbers</h4>A polynomial of degree $n\ge1$ with leading coefficient $a$ factors completely as
$$f(x)=a(x-c_1)(x-c_2)\cdots(x-c_n)$$
for complex numbers $c_1,\dots,c_n$ (not necessarily different). So it has <b>exactly $n$ roots, counting multiplicity</b>.</div>
<p>Example: $x^4-16=(x-2)(x+2)(x-2i)(x+2i)$: four roots. And $x^3-1=(x-1)\left(x-\frac{-1+i\sqrt3}{2}\right)\left(x-\frac{-1-i\sqrt3}{2}\right)$.</p>

<h3>2. Polynomials with real coefficients</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Conjugate Roots Theorem</h4>If $f(x)$ has <b>real coefficients</b> and $a+bi$ is a root, then its conjugate $a-bi$ is also a root.</div>
<p>Reason: taking conjugates of $f(z)=0$ gives $f(\bar z)=\bar0=0$, because conjugating doesn't change real coefficients. The theorem can fail without real coefficients: $x-i$ has the root $i$ but not $-i$.</p>
<p>Each conjugate pair combines into a real quadratic factor:</p>
$$(x-(a+bi))(x-(a-bi))=x^2-2ax+(a^2+b^2).$$
<p>For example, $1\pm2i$ give $x^2-2x+5$.</p>
<div class="box"><h4>Factorization over the real numbers</h4>Every polynomial with real coefficients is a product of linear factors and <b>irreducible quadratic</b> factors (quadratics with negative discriminant), all with real coefficients.</div>
<p>Consequences:</p>
<ul><li>Non-real roots come in pairs, so the number of non-real roots (counted with multiplicity) is even.</li>
<li>A real polynomial of <b>odd</b> degree always has at least one real root.</li>
<li>$x^4-16=(x-2)(x+2)(x^2+4)$ over the reals.</li></ul>

<h3>3. Building polynomials from roots</h3>
<p>Find the polynomial of least degree with real coefficients, leading coefficient $1$, and roots $2$ and $3-i$: the conjugate $3+i$ must also be a root, so</p>
$$f(x)=(x-2)(x^2-6x+10)=x^3-8x^2+22x-20.$$
<p>Shortcut for products: for a monic polynomial of degree $n$, the constant term is $(-1)^n$ times the product of the roots.</p>

<h3>4. Using a known complex root</h3>
<p>If $1+2i$ is a root of $x^3-3x^2+7x-5$ (real coefficients), then so is $1-2i$, so $x^2-2x+5$ is a factor. Dividing gives $x-1$, so the third root is $1$.</p>

<h3>Worked examples</h3>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 1.</b> A polynomial with real coefficients has degree $5$. How many real roots can it have (counting multiplicity)?
<details><summary>Solution</summary>Non-real roots come in pairs, so there are $0$, $2$, or $4$ non-real roots, leaving $5$, $3$, or $1$ real roots. Never $0$, $2$, or $4$.</details></div>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 2.</b> A monic quartic with real coefficients has roots $2i$ and $1+i$. What is its constant term?
<details><summary>Solution</summary>All four roots are $\pm2i$ and $1\pm i$. The constant term is the product of the roots: $(2i)(-2i)(1+i)(1-i)=4\cdot2=8$.</details></div>
<div class="warn"><h4>Common traps</h4><ul>
<li>Forgetting the conjugate when building a real polynomial from a complex root (the degree comes out too small).</li>
<li>Applying the Conjugate Roots Theorem when the coefficients are not real.</li>
<li>Confusing "exactly $n$ complex roots" with "$n$ real roots."</li>
</ul></div>

Q: A monic quartic polynomial with real coefficients has roots $2i$ and $1+i$. What is its constant term?
L: 2
A: 8
W: 4
W: 2
W: -8
W: 2+2i
S: Real coefficients force the conjugates $-2i$ and $1-i$ to be roots too, so all four roots are $\pm2i,\ 1\pm i$.
For a monic polynomial of even degree, the constant term equals the product of the roots.
$(2i)(-2i)=4$ and $(1+i)(1-i)=2$, so the product is $8$.
(Equivalently, $f(x)=(x^2+4)(x^2-2x+2)$, whose constant term is $4\cdot2=8$.)

Q: Which statement must be true for every polynomial of degree $7$ with real coefficients?
L: 1
A: \text{It has at least one real root.}
W: \text{It has exactly 7 real roots.}
W: \text{It has no real roots.}
W: \text{It has an even number of real roots.}
W: \text{All of its roots are real.}
S: Non-real roots of a real polynomial come in conjugate pairs, so there's an even number of them.
$7$ total roots minus an even number leaves an odd number of real roots, so at least $1$.
(Graphically: odd degree means the ends go in opposite directions, so the graph must cross the $x$-axis.)
