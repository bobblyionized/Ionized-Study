<h2>4.3 Real Roots of Polynomials</h2>
<p>Linear and quadratic equations have formulas. For higher degrees we combine three tools: the <b>Rational Root Test</b> to list candidates, the <b>Factor Theorem</b> (with synthetic division) to peel off roots we find, and the <b>Bounds Test</b> to know when we've found them all.</p>

<h3>1. The Rational Root Test</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Rational Root Test</h4>If $f(x)=a_nx^n+\cdots+a_1x+a_0$ has <b>integer coefficients</b> and $\frac rs$ (in lowest terms) is a rational root, then
$$r\ \text{divides the constant term } a_0\quad\text{and}\quad s\ \text{divides the leading coefficient } a_n.$$</div>
<p>For $f(x)=2x^4+x^3-17x^2-4x+6$: $r\in\{\pm1,\pm2,\pm3,\pm6\}$ and $s\in\{1,2\}$, so the only possible rational roots are</p>
$$\pm1,\ \pm2,\ \pm3,\ \pm6,\ \pm\tfrac12,\ \pm\tfrac32.$$
<p>Testing them (by substitution or synthetic division) shows that $-3$ and $\frac12$ are roots. The test only lists <i>candidates</i>; most candidates fail, and irrational roots never appear on the list.</p>
<div class="tip"><h4>Monic polynomials</h4>If the leading coefficient is $1$, every rational root is an <b>integer</b> dividing the constant term. So $x^3+x+3$ could only have rational roots $\pm1,\pm3$; none of them work, so it has no rational roots at all.</div>
<p><b>Why it's true:</b> substitute $x=\frac rs$ and multiply by $s^n$: $a_nr^n+a_{n-1}r^{n-1}s+\cdots+a_0s^n=0$. Every term except the last is divisible by $r$, so $r\mid a_0s^n$, and since $\gcd(r,s)=1$, $r\mid a_0$. The same argument with the first term gives $s\mid a_n$.</p>

<h3>2. Finding all the roots</h3>
<ol><li>List the rational candidates and test them. (A quick graph helps you choose which to test.)</li>
<li>Each root $c$ you find gives a factor $x-c$; divide it out with synthetic division.</li>
<li>Keep going on the quotient. Once it's quadratic, use factoring or the quadratic formula; this also catches irrational roots.</li></ol>
<p>Example: $2x^3-5x^2-x+6$. Candidates $\pm1,\pm2,\pm3,\pm6,\pm\frac12,\pm\frac32$. $f(2)=16-20-2+6=0$, so divide by $x-2$: $2x^2-x-3=(2x-3)(x+1)$. Roots: $2,\ \frac32,\ -1$.</p>
<p>Example with irrational roots: $x^3-4x^2+2x+3$ has $f(3)=0$; the quotient $x^2-x-1$ gives $\frac{1\pm\sqrt5}{2}$.</p>
<p>Biquadratics like $x^4-5x^2+4$ factor as a quadratic in $x^2$: $(x^2-1)(x^2-4)$, roots $\pm1,\pm2$.</p>

<h3>3. The Bounds Test</h3>
<p>A number $d$ is an <b>upper bound</b> for the real roots if every real root is $\le d$, and a <b>lower bound</b> if every real root is $\ge d$. Bounds tell you a graph in the window between them shows every $x$-intercept.</p>
<div class="box"><h4>Bounds Test</h4>Let $f$ have a <b>positive leading coefficient</b>, and divide $f(x)$ by $x-d$ synthetically.
<ul><li>If $d\gt0$ and every number in the bottom row is <b>nonnegative</b>, then $d$ is an upper bound.</li>
<li>If $d\lt0$ and the numbers in the bottom row <b>alternate in sign</b> (zeros may count as either sign), then $d$ is a lower bound.</li></ul></div>
<p>Why the upper bound works: if all quotient coefficients and the remainder are $\ge0$, then for any $x\gt d$, $f(x)=(x-d)q(x)+r$ is a positive number times a positive number plus a nonnegative number, so it can't be $0$.</p>
<p>If the test fails (some negative entry), no conclusion can be drawn: try a larger $d$.</p>

<h3>Worked examples</h3>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 1.</b> Show that $3$ is an upper bound for the roots of $x^3-2x^2-x+1$.
<details><summary>Solution</summary>Synthetic division by $x-3$: coefficients $1,-2,-1,1$; bottom row $1,\,1,\,2,\,7$. All nonnegative, so no root exceeds $3$.</details></div>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 2.</b> Which of $\frac32$, $\frac23$, $4$ could be a rational root of $3x^3-x^2+5x+4$?
<details><summary>Solution</summary>Numerators must divide $4$ and denominators must divide $3$: candidates $\pm1,\pm2,\pm4,\pm\frac13,\pm\frac23,\pm\frac43$. So $\frac23$ and $4$ are candidates, but $\frac32$ (denominator $2$) is impossible.</details></div>
<div class="warn"><h4>Common traps</h4><ul>
<li>Swapping the roles: the numerator divides the <b>constant</b>, the denominator divides the <b>leading</b> coefficient.</li>
<li>Forgetting the $\pm$ signs.</li>
<li>Applying the test to non-integer coefficients (clear fractions first).</li>
<li>Concluding "no upper bound" when the Bounds Test fails. It's only a sufficient condition.</li>
</ul></div>

L: 2
