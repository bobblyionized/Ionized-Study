<h2>4.4 Graphs of Polynomial Functions</h2>
<p>You can sketch a surprisingly accurate polynomial graph with no calculator: the leading term tells you the ends, the factors tell you the $x$-intercepts and what happens at each one, and the degree limits how many bumps there can be.</p>

<h3>1. Power functions $y=ax^n$</h3>
<ul><li><b>$n$ odd:</b> shaped like $x^3$. If $a\gt0$ it falls on the left and rises on the right; if $a\lt0$, the reverse.</li>
<li><b>$n$ even:</b> shaped like $x^2$ (flatter near $0$, steeper far out as $n$ grows). If $a\gt0$ both ends rise; if $a\lt0$ both ends fall.</li></ul>
<div class="lfigs">
<div class="lplot" data-f="0.3*x*x*x;-0.3*x*x*x" data-x="-3,3" data-y="-5,5" data-w="170" data-cap="odd degree: $\pm0.3x^3$"></div>
<div class="lplot" data-f="0.3*x*x*x*x;-0.3*x*x*x*x" data-x="-3,3" data-y="-5,5" data-w="170" data-cap="even degree: $\pm0.3x^4$"></div>
</div>

<h3>2. Continuity and smoothness</h3>
<p>Every polynomial graph is <b>continuous</b> (no jumps, gaps, or holes) and <b>smooth</b> (no sharp corners). A graph with a corner, like $|x|$, or a jump, like $\lfloor x\rfloor$, can't be a polynomial.</p>

<h3>3. End behavior</h3>
<div class="box">When $|x|$ is large, the graph of a polynomial closely resembles the graph of its <b>leading term</b>. So:
<ul><li>odd degree: one end up, the other down;</li>
<li>even degree: both ends up (positive leading coefficient) or both down (negative).</li></ul></div>
<p>For $f(x)=2x^3+x^2-6x$ at $x=100$, the leading term $2{,}000{,}000$ dwarfs $x^2-6x=9400$. For a factored polynomial, multiply the leading terms of the factors: $(2x-1)^3(x+4)^2$ behaves like $8x^3\cdot x^2=8x^5$.</p>

<h3>4. $x$-intercepts and multiplicity</h3>
<p>A polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n$ $x$-intercepts. A root $c$ has <b>multiplicity $k$</b> if $(x-c)^k$ is a factor but $(x-c)^{k+1}$ is not.</p>
<div class="box"><h4>Multiplicity and graphs</h4><ul>
<li>Odd multiplicity: the graph <b>crosses</b> the $x$-axis at $c$ (flattening out, like $x^3$, if $k\ge3$).</li>
<li>Even multiplicity: the graph <b>touches</b> the $x$-axis at $c$ and turns back, without crossing.</li></ul></div>
<div class="lfigs"><div class="lplot" data-f="0.15*(x-1)*(x-1)*(x+2)*(x+2)*(x+2)" data-x="-4,3" data-y="-4,6" data-w="230" data-cap="$(x-1)^2(x+2)^3$: touches at $1$, crosses (flattening) at $-2$"></div></div>
<p>Irreducible quadratic factors like $x^2+1$ contribute no $x$-intercepts: $x(x^2+1)(x-3)^2$ has only the intercepts $0$ and $3$.</p>

<h3>5. Bumps: local extrema and inflection points</h3>
<div class="box">A polynomial of degree $n$ has <b>at most $n-1$</b> local extrema (peaks and valleys) and at most $n-2$ points of inflection (where the curve changes between bending up and bending down).</div>
<p>So a cubic has at most $2$ turning points and a quartic at most $3$. A graph with $4$ turning points needs degree at least $5$. Note: odd-degree graphs have an even number of turning points ($0,2,4,\dots$), and even-degree graphs an odd number.</p>

<h3>6. Sketching from factors: a sign chart</h3>
<p>The roots split the line into intervals where $f$ keeps one sign. For $f(x)=(x+1)(x-2)^2$: to the right of $2$ all factors are positive; crossing $2$ (even multiplicity) the sign stays the same; crossing $-1$ (odd) it flips. So $f\gt0$ on $(-1,2)\cup(2,\infty)$ and $f\lt0$ on $(-\infty,-1)$.</p>

<h3>7. Complete graphs and applications</h3>
<p>A <b>complete graph</b> shows every intercept, every peak and valley, and the end behavior. If a calculator window shows $n$ $x$-intercepts and $n-1$ turning points for a degree-$n$ polynomial, nothing is hidden. Applied models (box volumes, revenue, population) usually use only the part of the graph on a realistic domain.</p>

<h3>Worked examples</h3>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 1.</b> Find the polynomial of least degree whose graph touches the $x$-axis at $2$, crosses it at $-1$, and passes through $(0,8)$.
<details><summary>Solution</summary>Touch at $2$ needs an even power, cross at $-1$ an odd one; least degree uses $(x-2)^2(x+1)$. Then $f(x)=a(x-2)^2(x+1)$ and $f(0)=4a=8$, so $a=2$: $f(x)=2(x-2)^2(x+1)$, degree $3$.</details></div>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 2.</b> Describe the end behavior of $f(x)=-2x^5+7x^2-1$.
<details><summary>Solution</summary>Leading term $-2x^5$: odd degree, negative coefficient. The graph rises on the far left ($f\to\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$) and falls on the far right.</details></div>
<div class="warn"><h4>Common traps</h4><ul>
<li>Judging the end behavior by the first term written instead of the highest power.</li>
<li>Thinking every root crosses the axis. Even multiplicity bounces.</li>
<li>Counting the number of turning points as exactly $n-1$. It's an upper limit.</li>
</ul></div>
