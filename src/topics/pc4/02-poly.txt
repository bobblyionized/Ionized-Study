<script type="text/plain" id="L-pc4-poly">
<h2>4.2 Polynomial Functions</h2>
<p>A <b>polynomial</b> is an expression $a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1x+a_0$ where $n$ is a nonnegative integer and the $a_i$ are constants called <b>coefficients</b>. If $a_n\ne0$, then $n$ is the <b>degree</b>, $a_n$ is the <b>leading coefficient</b>, and $a_0$ is the <b>constant term</b>. Constants are degree-$0$ polynomials; the <b>zero polynomial</b> has no degree.</p>
<ul><li>$x^3-\sqrt2x+\frac12$ is a polynomial of degree $3$. $\ x^{-2}+1$, $\sqrt x$, and $\frac1x$ are <b>not</b> polynomials (exponents must be whole numbers).</li>
<li>The degree of a product is the <b>sum</b> of the degrees, and the leading coefficient of a product is the product of the leading coefficients: $(2x^3-1)(x^2+4x)^2$ has degree $3+4=7$ and leading coefficient $2\cdot1^2=2$.</li>
<li>Useful values: $f(0)$ is the constant term, and $f(1)$ is the <b>sum of the coefficients</b>.</li></ul>

<h3>1. Polynomial division and the Division Algorithm</h3>
<p>Polynomial long division works just like numerical long division. Divide $3x^4-2x^3+x-5$ by $x^2+1$ (write in the missing $0x^2$):</p>
<ul><li>$3x^4\div x^2=3x^2$. Subtract $3x^2(x^2+1)=3x^4+3x^2$: left with $-2x^3-3x^2+x-5$.</li>
<li>$-2x^3\div x^2=-2x$. Subtract $-2x^3-2x$: left with $-3x^2+3x-5$.</li>
<li>$-3x^2\div x^2=-3$. Subtract $-3x^2-3$: left with $3x-2$, whose degree is less than $2$, so we stop.</li></ul>
<p>Quotient $3x^2-2x-3$, remainder $3x-2$.</p>
<div class="box"><h4>Division Algorithm</h4>If $f(x)$ is divided by a nonzero polynomial $g(x)$, there are unique polynomials $q(x)$ (quotient) and $r(x)$ (remainder) with
$$f(x)=g(x)\,q(x)+r(x),$$
where $r(x)=0$ or $\deg r\lt\deg g$.</div>
<p>Dividing by a linear $x-c$ always leaves a constant remainder; dividing by a quadratic leaves a remainder of the form $ax+b$.</p>

<h3>2. The Remainder Theorem</h3>
<div class="box">The remainder when $f(x)$ is divided by $x-c$ is $f(c)$.</div>
<p>Proof in one line: $f(x)=(x-c)q(x)+r$; set $x=c$ to get $f(c)=r$. This turns division questions into evaluation. The remainder of $x^{100}+x^{50}+1$ divided by $x+1=x-(-1)$ is $f(-1)=1+1+1=3$, without dividing anything.</p>
<p><b>Remainder by a quadratic:</b> if $f(1)=3$ and $f(2)=5$, the remainder on division by $(x-1)(x-2)$ is $ax+b$ with $a+b=3$ and $2a+b=5$, so it's $2x+1$.</p>

<h3>3. Roots and the Factor Theorem</h3>
<p>A number $c$ is a <b>root</b> (or <b>zero</b>) of $f$ if $f(c)=0$. Real roots are the $x$-intercepts of the graph.</p>
<div class="box"><h4>Factor Theorem</h4>$x-c$ is a factor of $f(x)$ <b>if and only if</b> $c$ is a root of $f$, i.e. $f(c)=0$.</div>
<ul><li>Is $x+3$ a factor of $x^3+27$? $f(-3)=-27+27=0$: yes. ($x^3+27=(x+3)(x^2-3x+9)$.)</li>
<li>Find $k$ so $x-2$ divides $x^3+kx^2-4x+8$: $f(2)=8+4k-8+8=4k+8=0$, so $k=-2$.</li>
<li>Knowing one root lets you factor: $x^3-2x^2-5x+6$ has root $1$ (coefficients sum to $0$), so dividing by $x-1$ gives $x^2-x-6=(x-3)(x+2)$, and $f(x)=(x-1)(x-3)(x+2)$.</li></ul>

<h3>4. How many roots?</h3>
<div class="box">A polynomial of degree $n$ has <b>at most $n$</b> distinct roots.</div>
<p>Each root $c$ contributes a factor $x-c$, and a product of more than $n$ linear factors would have degree more than $n$. It may have fewer real roots: $x^2+1$ has none.</p>

<h3>5. Building a polynomial from its roots</h3>
<p>The polynomials of degree $n$ with roots $r_1,\dots,r_n$ are exactly $f(x)=a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)\cdots(x-r_n)$, $a\ne0$. One extra condition determines $a$. Degree $3$ with roots $1,-2,3$ and $f(0)=12$: $f(0)=a(-1)(2)(-3)=6a=12$, so $a=2$ and $f(x)=2(x-1)(x+2)(x-3)$.</p>

<h3>Worked examples</h3>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 1.</b> Find the remainder when $2x^3-3x^2+4x-7$ is divided by $x-2$.
<details><summary>Solution</summary>By the Remainder Theorem it's $f(2)=16-12+8-7=5$.</details></div>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 2.</b> When $f(x)$ is divided by $x-3$ the remainder is $7$. What is the remainder when $f(x)+x^2$ is divided by $x-3$?
<details><summary>Solution</summary>Evaluate at $3$: $f(3)+9=7+9=16$.</details></div>
<div class="tip"><h4>Strategy</h4>Any question about a remainder on division by $x-c$, or about whether $x-c$ is a factor, is really a question about the number $f(c)$. Evaluate instead of dividing.</div>
<div class="warn"><h4>Common traps</h4><ul>
<li>Dividing by $x+3$ means $c=-3$, not $3$.</li>
<li>Forgetting zero placeholders (like $0x^2$) in long division.</li>
<li>Thinking a degree-$n$ polynomial always has $n$ real roots.</li>
</ul></div>

A: 3x^2+\dfrac{2}{x}
W: \sqrt5\,x^3-\pi
W: \dfrac{x^4-1}{2}
W: 7

S: A polynomial's terms must be constants times <b>whole-number</b> powers of $x$.
• $\sqrt5x^3-\pi$: coefficients can be any real numbers. Polynomial.
• $\frac{x^4-1}{2}=\frac12x^4-\frac12$. Polynomial.
• $7$: a constant (degree $0$). Polynomial.
• $(x+1)^{10}$: expands to a polynomial of degree $10$.
• $\frac2x=2x^{-1}$ has a negative exponent. <b>Not</b> a polynomial.
