<h2>4.5.A Other Rational Functions</h2>
<p>When the numerator of a rational function has <b>larger</b> degree than the denominator, there is no horizontal asymptote. Polynomial division tells us what the graph does instead.</p>

<h3>1. The key idea: divide</h3>
<p>By the Division Algorithm, $g(x)=h(x)q(x)+r(x)$ with $\deg r\lt\deg h$. Dividing by $h(x)$:</p>
$$\frac{g(x)}{h(x)}=q(x)+\frac{r(x)}{h(x)}.$$
<p>Since $\deg r\lt\deg h$, the fraction $\frac{r(x)}{h(x)}$ is <b>little</b> when $|x|$ is big (Big-Little Principle). So for large $|x|$ the graph gets closer and closer to the graph of the <b>quotient</b> $y=q(x)$.</p>
<div class="box"><ul>
<li>Numerator degree = denominator degree $+1$: the quotient is linear, so the graph has an <b>oblique (slant) asymptote</b> $y=mx+b$.</li>
<li>Numerator degree $\ge$ denominator degree $+2$: the graph approaches the polynomial $y=q(x)$ (a parabola, a cubic, ...).</li></ul></div>

<h3>2. Oblique asymptotes</h3>
<p>$f(x)=\dfrac{x^3+3x^2+x+1}{x^2+2x-1}$. Long division gives quotient $x+1$ and remainder $2$:</p>
$$f(x)=(x+1)+\frac{2}{x^2+2x-1}.$$
<p>The oblique asymptote is $y=x+1$. The graph also has two vertical asymptotes, at the roots of $x^2+2x-1$, namely $x=-1\pm\sqrt2$.</p>
<p>A quick case: $\dfrac{x^2+1}{x}=x+\dfrac1x$, so the oblique asymptote is $y=x$ (and the vertical asymptote is $x=0$).</p>
<div class="lfigs"><div class="lplot" data-f="(x*x+1)/x;x" data-x="-6,6" data-y="-6,6" data-w="230" data-cap="$\frac{x^2+1}{x}$ approaches the line $y=x$"></div></div>
<p>Dividing by a linear factor is fastest with synthetic division: $\dfrac{2x^2-3x+4}{x-1}$: coefficients $2,-3,4$, $c=1$, bottom row $2,-1\mid3$. So $f(x)=2x-1+\frac{3}{x-1}$, oblique asymptote $y=2x-1$.</p>

<h3>3. Polynomial asymptotes of higher degree</h3>
<p>$g(x)=\dfrac{x^3+2x^2-7x+5}{x-1}=(x^2+3x-4)+\dfrac{1}{x-1}$. For large $|x|$ the graph hugs the parabola $y=x^2+3x-4$; near $x=1$ it shoots off along the vertical asymptote.</p>
<p>$h(x)=\dfrac{x^5-5x^3+4x+1}{x^2-1}=x^3-4x+\dfrac{1}{x^2-1}$ approaches the cubic $y=x^3-4x$.</p>

<h3>4. Graphing procedure</h3>
<ol><li>Find the domain; reduce to lowest terms; locate <b>holes</b>, <b>vertical asymptotes</b>, and <b>intercepts</b>.</li>
<li>Divide to find the asymptote for large $|x|$ ($y=0$, $y=\frac ac$, a slanted line, or a polynomial).</li>
<li>Decide which side of the asymptote the graph is on using the sign of $\frac{r(x)}{h(x)}$, plot a few points, and sketch.</li></ol>
<div class="warn"><h4>Where does the graph sit relative to the slant asymptote?</h4>The difference $f(x)-q(x)=\frac{r(x)}{h(x)}$. For $x+\frac1x$: when $x\gt0$ the graph is above $y=x$; when $x\lt0$ it's below.</div>

<h3>Worked examples</h3>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 1.</b> Find the oblique asymptote of $f(x)=\dfrac{3x^2-x+2}{x+1}$.
<details><summary>Solution</summary>Synthetic division by $x+1$ ($c=-1$): $3,-1,2\to3,-4\mid6$. So $f(x)=3x-4+\frac{6}{x+1}$; the asymptote is $y=3x-4$.</details></div>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 2.</b> Does $\dfrac{x^2+4}{x^2-1}$ have an oblique asymptote?
<details><summary>Solution</summary>No: the degrees are equal, so it has the horizontal asymptote $y=1$ instead. Oblique asymptotes require the numerator's degree to be exactly one more.</details></div>
<div class="tip"><h4>Shortcut for the slope</h4>If $\deg g=\deg h+1$, the slope of the oblique asymptote is the ratio of leading coefficients. You still need division to get the intercept.</div>

Q: Which function has an oblique (slant) asymptote?
L: 1
A: \dfrac{x^3-1}{x^2+4}
W: \dfrac{x^2+4}{x^2-1}
W: \dfrac{x^4+1}{x^2}
W: \dfrac{x+5}{x^2-9}
W: \dfrac{2x-1}{x+3}
S: An oblique asymptote occurs exactly when the numerator's degree is <b>one more</b> than the denominator's.
• $\frac{x^3-1}{x^2+4}$: degrees $3$ and $2$. ✓ (It approaches $y=x$.)
• $\frac{x^2+4}{x^2-1}$ and $\frac{2x-1}{x+3}$: equal degrees, horizontal asymptotes.
• $\frac{x+5}{x^2-9}$: smaller numerator degree, asymptote $y=0$.
• $\frac{x^4+1}{x^2}$: degrees differ by $2$; it approaches the parabola $y=x^2$, not a line.

Q: The graph of $h(x)=\dfrac{x^5-5x^3+4x+1}{x^2-1}$ approaches which polynomial for large $|x|$?
L: 2
A: y=x^3-4x
W: y=x^3-5x
W: y=x^3
W: y=x^3-4x+1
W: y=x^5
S: Long division by $x^2-1$ (fill in zeros: $x^5+0x^4-5x^3+0x^2+4x+1$):
$x^5\div x^2=x^3$; subtract $x^5-x^3$: left $-4x^3+4x+1$.
$-4x^3\div x^2=-4x$; subtract $-4x^3+4x$: left $1$.
So $h(x)=x^3-4x+\dfrac{1}{x^2-1}$, and the graph approaches $y=x^3-4x$.

Q: The function $f(x)=\dfrac{2x^2+5x-3}{x+3}$ has ...
L: 2
A: \text{a hole at }x=-3\text{ and no vertical asymptote}
W: \text{a vertical asymptote at }x=-3
W: \text{an oblique asymptote }y=2x-1\text{ and a vertical asymptote }x=-3
W: \text{a horizontal asymptote }y=2
W: \text{a hole at }x=\tfrac12
S: Factor the numerator: $2x^2+5x-3=(2x-1)(x+3)$.
So $f(x)=\dfrac{(2x-1)(x+3)}{x+3}=2x-1$ for $x\ne-3$.
The factor cancels: a <b>hole</b> at $x=-3$ (height $-7$), no vertical asymptote. The graph is just the line $y=2x-1$ with a point missing, so it's not a case of a separate oblique asymptote.
