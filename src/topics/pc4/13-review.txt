<h3>4.1 Quadratic functions</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Key facts</h4><ul>
<li><b>Vertex form</b> $f(x)=a(x-h)^2+k$ has vertex $(h,k)$. Watch the sign: $(x+1)^2$ means $h=-1$.</li>
<li><b>Standard form</b> $f(x)=ax^2+bx+c$ has vertex at $x=-\dfrac{b}{2a}$; plug that $x$ in to get the $y$-coordinate.</li>
<li>$a\gt0$: opens up, the vertex is the minimum. $a\lt0$: opens down, the vertex is the maximum. $|a|$ controls how narrow it is.</li>
<li>$y=ax^2+k$ is $y=ax^2$ shifted <b>up</b> $k$; $y=a(x-h)^2$ is shifted <b>right</b> $h$.</li></ul></div>
<p><b>Where $-\frac b{2a}$ comes from:</b> completing the square, $ax^2+bx+c=a\left(x+\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2+c-\frac{b^2}{4a}$, so the vertex is at $x=-\frac b{2a}$.</p>
<p><b>Optimization ("largest area", "maximum revenue", "maximum height").</b> Write the quantity as a quadratic in one variable, then the answer is at the vertex.</p>
<ul><li><i>Fencing:</i> name the side lengths with one variable, use the fixed amount of fence to write the other side, then area $=$ length $\times$ width.</li>
<li><i>Price/quantity:</i> if each step of $k$ changes the price by $p$ and the quantity by $q$, revenue $=$ (price) $\times$ (quantity), both written in terms of $k$.</li>
<li><i>Projectile:</i> $h(t)=h_0+v_0t-16t^2$. The launch height is $h(0)$; the maximum is at $t=-\frac{b}{2a}$; "back at the launch height" means $h(t)=h(0)$.</li></ul>

<h3>4.2 Polynomials and division</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>What counts as a polynomial</h4>A sum of terms $ax^n$ with $n$ a <b>whole number</b> ($0,1,2,\dots$). Constants like $\pi$, $\sqrt2$, or $2^3$ are fine as coefficients. Not allowed: $\frac1x$ ($x^{-1}$), $\sqrt x$ ($x^{1/2}$), $|x|$, or fractional exponents that don't simplify to whole numbers. ($x^{120/10}=x^{12}$ is fine.)</div>
<p><b>Division Algorithm.</b> Dividing $f(x)$ by $g(x)$ gives $f(x)=g(x)q(x)+r(x)$ with the remainder $r$ of lower degree than $g$. Use long division for divisors like $x^2+1$; keep a $0$ placeholder for every missing power.</p>
<p><b>Remainder Theorem.</b> The remainder when $f(x)$ is divided by $x-c$ is $f(c)$. (From $f(x)=(x-c)q(x)+r$, plug in $x=c$.) So to find the remainder of a huge polynomial divided by $x-1$, just compute $f(1)$.</p>
<p><b>Factor Theorem.</b> $x-c$ is a factor of $f(x)$ exactly when $f(c)=0$.</p>
<p><b>Synthetic division</b> (only for divisors $x-c$): write $c$ and the coefficients (with $0$s for missing powers), bring down the first, then repeatedly multiply by $c$ and add. The last number is the remainder $f(c)$; the others are the quotient's coefficients, one degree lower.</p>
<p><b>Building a polynomial from roots.</b> Roots $r_1,\dots,r_n$ give $f(x)=a(x-r_1)\cdots(x-r_n)$; use one extra condition (like $f(0)=5$) to find $a$ or a remaining factor.</p>
<p><b>Multiplicity.</b> In $(x-c)^m$, the root $c$ has multiplicity $m$. Factor completely first: $(x^2-9)^2=(x-3)^2(x+3)^2$.</p>
<p><b>Difference quotient.</b> $\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$: expand $f(x+h)$, subtract, and every remaining term has a factor of $h$ to cancel. Use $(x+h)^3=x^3+3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3$.</p>

<h3>4.3 Real roots</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Rational Root Test</h4>If $f$ has integer coefficients, every rational root $\frac pq$ (in lowest terms) has $p$ dividing the constant term and $q$ dividing the leading coefficient.</div>
<p><b>Method for finding all roots:</b></p>
<ol><li>Factor out any common factor (like $3y^3$), and look for quadratic form: $x^4-11x^2+18$ is a quadratic in $x^2$.</li>
<li>List the possible rational roots and test them (synthetic division is fastest). Use any hint given (like "$2$ is a root").</li>
<li>Each root found lets you divide down one degree. Once you reach a quadratic, use factoring or the quadratic formula.</li>
<li>Count real roots by factoring: $x^3+4x=x(x^2+4)$ has one real root, since $x^2+4$ is never $0$.</li></ol>
<p>A polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n$ real roots. If no rational roots work, the remaining roots can still be irrational; the Intermediate Value Theorem says a sign change between two consecutive integers means a root lies between them.</p>

<h3>4.4 Graphs of polynomials</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Reading and sketching polynomial graphs</h4><ul>
<li><b>End behavior</b> follows the leading term $ax^n$: even degree means both ends go the same way; odd degree means opposite ways ($a\gt0$: rises on the right).</li>
<li>A degree-$n$ polynomial has at most $n$ $x$-intercepts and at most $n-1$ turning points. So a graph with $4$ turning points has degree at least $5$.</li>
<li>At a root of <b>odd</b> multiplicity the graph crosses the axis; at <b>even</b> multiplicity it touches and turns back.</li>
<li>Polynomial graphs are smooth and unbroken: no gaps, holes, jumps, sharp corners, or asymptotes.</li></ul></div>
<p>To sketch: find intercepts (factor), check multiplicities, use the end behavior, and locate turning points (with a calculator, or from the derivative-free fact that a turning point lies between consecutive roots).</p>

<h3>4.5 Rational functions</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>The checklist for $f(x)=\dfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}$</h4><ol>
<li><b>Factor</b> the top and bottom completely.</li>
<li><b>Holes:</b> a factor that cancels leaves a hole there. Find its $y$-value by plugging that $x$ into the simplified function.</li>
<li><b>Vertical asymptotes:</b> zeros of the denominator that remain after cancelling.</li>
<li><b>$x$-intercepts:</b> zeros of the numerator that remain after cancelling. <b>$y$-intercept:</b> $f(0)$.</li>
<li><b>Horizontal asymptote</b> (compare degrees of top $n$ and bottom $m$): $n\lt m$ gives $y=0$; $n=m$ gives $y=\frac{\text{leading coefficient of top}}{\text{leading coefficient of bottom}}$; $n\gt m$ gives none.</li></ol></div>
<p><b>4.5.A: when the top's degree is exactly one more</b>, divide: the quotient (a line) is a slant asymptote. If the top's degree is bigger by more, the graph follows the quotient polynomial's shape for large $|x|$.</p>
<p><b>Sign of a rational function on an interval:</b> pick a test value and check the sign of each factor. Factors that are always positive, like $x^2+1$, never change the sign.</p>

<h3>4.8 Theory of equations</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Key theorems</h4><ul>
<li><b>Fundamental Theorem of Algebra:</b> every polynomial of degree $n\ge1$ has exactly $n$ complex roots, counting multiplicity, so it factors completely into linear factors over the complex numbers.</li>
<li><b>Conjugate Roots Theorem:</b> if a polynomial has <b>real</b> coefficients and $a+bi$ is a root, so is $a-bi$.</li>
<li>So a real polynomial with roots $a\pm bi$ has the real quadratic factor $(x-(a+bi))(x-(a-bi))=x^2-2ax+(a^2+b^2)$.</li></ul></div>
<p><b>Solving in the complex numbers.</b> Use the quadratic formula; a negative discriminant gives $\sqrt{-d}=i\sqrt d$. Quartics like $3x^4+x^2-2$ are quadratics in $x^2$: solve for $x^2$, then take square roots (a negative $x^2$ gives imaginary roots).</p>
<p><b>Sum and difference of cubes:</b> $x^3+8=(x+2)(x^2-2x+4)$ and $x^3-27=(x-3)(x^2+3x+9)$. The quadratic factor gives the two complex roots.</p>
<p><b>Given one complex root.</b> If $i$ is a root of a real polynomial, so is $-i$, so $x^2+1$ is a factor. Divide by $x^2+1$ and factor the quotient.</p>
<p><b>Building a polynomial.</b> List every root, including the conjugates the theorem forces, multiply the factors, and use any extra condition to find the leading coefficient.</p>
<div class="tip"><b>Test tips:</b> show the vertex formula or the Rational Root list before computing; write each factorization step; and for rational functions, always factor and cancel before naming asymptotes.</div>

Root $0$ gives the factor $x$; one more factor is needed for degree $4$. Using $x$ again: $x^2(x^2-2x+2)=x^4-2x^3+2x^2$. (Many other answers work, like $x(x-5)(x^2-2x+2)$.)
$x^3-2x^2+2x$ has the right roots but is only degree $3$.

Q: <b>Book #96.</b> Find a fourth-degree polynomial $f$ whose only roots are $2+i$ and $2-i$, with $f(-1)=50$.
L: 3
A: \frac12\left(x^2-4x+5\right)^2
W: \left(x^2-4x+5\right)^2
W: 2\left(x^2-4x+5\right)^2
W: \frac12\left(x^2+4x+5\right)^2
W: \frac14\left(x^2-4x+5\right)^2
S: The roots $2\pm i$ give the factor $(x-2-i)(x-2+i)=(x-2)^2+1=x^2-4x+5$.
For degree $4$ with no other roots, each must be a double root: $f(x)=a(x^2-4x+5)^2$.
$f(-1)=a(1+4+5)^2=100a=50$, so $a=\frac12$: $f(x)=\frac12(x^2-4x+5)^2=\frac12x^4-4x^3+13x^2-20x+\frac{25}2$.
