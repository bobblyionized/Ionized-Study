<h2>4.4.A Polynomial Models</h2>
<p>Real data rarely follows a formula exactly, but a polynomial often fits it well enough to describe trends and make predictions. Calculators do this with <b>regression</b> (linear, quadratic, cubic, quartic), which finds the polynomial of a chosen degree that fits the data best. This lesson covers how to choose the degree, how exact fits work, and how to use and judge a model.</p>

<h3>1. Choosing the degree from the shape</h3>
<ul><li><b>Linear</b> ($ax+b$): steady increase or decrease.</li>
<li><b>Quadratic</b> ($ax^2+bx+c$): one turn, rising then falling or falling then rising.</li>
<li><b>Cubic</b>: up to two turns, e.g. rising, leveling or dipping, then rising again.</li>
<li><b>Quartic</b>: up to three turns.</li></ul>
<p>Use the lowest degree that captures the pattern. A higher degree always fits the given points at least as well, but it can wiggle wildly between and beyond them.</p>

<h3>2. Finite differences</h3>
<p>For equally spaced $x$-values, take differences of consecutive $y$-values, then differences of those, and so on.</p>
<div class="box">If the data come from a polynomial of degree $n$, the $n$th differences are <b>constant</b> (and the $(n+1)$st differences are $0$). For $ax^n+\cdots$ with spacing $1$, the constant $n$th difference is $a\cdot n!$.</div>
<table class="t" style="width:auto"><tr><th>$x$</th><td>0</td><td>1</td><td>2</td><td>3</td><td>4</td></tr><tr><th>$y$</th><td>3</td><td>5</td><td>11</td><td>21</td><td>35</td></tr><tr><th>1st diff</th><td></td><td>2</td><td>6</td><td>10</td><td>14</td></tr><tr><th>2nd diff</th><td></td><td></td><td>4</td><td>4</td><td>4</td></tr></table>
<p>Constant second differences: the data are quadratic, with $a\cdot2!=4$, so $a=2$. In fact $y=2x^2+3$. Differences also let you <b>extend</b> a table: the next first difference is $18$, so the next $y$ is $35+18=53$.</p>

<h3>3. Exact fits through points</h3>
<p>Through any $n+1$ points with different $x$-values there is exactly one polynomial of degree at most $n$. Two points determine a line, three a quadratic, five a quartic. To find it, substitute each point and solve the system. Through $(0,3),(1,5),(2,11)$: $c=3$, $a+b+3=5$, $4a+2b+3=11$, giving $a=2,b=0$.</p>

<h3>4. Using a model</h3>
<ul><li><b>Predicting:</b> substitute the input. If $x=0$ is 1990, then the year 2005 is $x=15$.</li>
<li><b>Solving:</b> to find when the model reaches a value, solve $f(x)=$ value (graphically or algebraically).</li>
<li><b>Residuals:</b> residual $=$ actual $-$ predicted. Small residuals mean a good fit for those points.</li>
<li><b>Extrapolation is risky.</b> A model that fits 1990–2005 can give absurd values for 2050 (negative populations, infinite growth). Predictions far outside the data are guesses.</li></ul>

<h3>5. Geometric models</h3>
<p>Some polynomial models come from geometry, not data. Cutting $x\times x$ squares from the corners of a $44\times28$ sheet and folding gives a box of volume $V(x)=x(44-2x)(28-2x)$, a cubic, valid for $0\lt x\lt14$. Its maximum (about $3086$ cubic inches at $x\approx5.57$) is found with a calculator's maximum finder.</p>

<h3>Worked examples</h3>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 1.</b> The values of a polynomial at $x=0,1,2,3,4$ are $1,2,9,28,65$. What degree is it?
<details><summary>Solution</summary>First differences $1,7,19,37$; second $6,12,18$; third $6,6$. Third differences are constant, so it's a cubic (in fact $x^3+1$).</details></div>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 2.</b> A model predicts $y=0.5x^2-2x+10$. The actual value at $x=6$ was $13.2$. What is the residual?
<details><summary>Solution</summary>Predicted: $18-12+10=16$. Residual $=13.2-16=-2.8$ (the model overestimated).</details></div>
<div class="tip"><h4>How many points pin down a polynomial?</h4>Degree $n$ has $n+1$ coefficients, so it takes $n+1$ conditions. That's why a quartic needs $5$ data points for an exact fit.</div>
