<h2>3.3 Graphs of Functions</h2>
<p>The <b>graph of a function $f$</b> is the graph of the equation $y=f(x)$: the set of all points $(x,f(x))$ with $x$ in the domain. Every fact about a function can be read off its graph: $f(a)$ is the height of the graph above $x=a$, the domain is the graph's shadow on the $x$-axis, and the range is its shadow on the $y$-axis.</p>
<p>Calculators draw graphs quickly, but their graphs can be incomplete or misleading (pixelation, hidden wiggles, missing endpoints). Use what you know algebraically first, and treat a calculator picture as something to be checked.</p>

<h3>1. The catalog of basic functions</h3>
<p>Memorize these shapes. Almost every graph in this course is built from them by the transformations of Section 3.4.</p>

</div>
<p>$\sqrt x$ lies entirely in the first quadrant because its inputs and outputs are both $\ge0$. $x^2$ and $|x|$ are symmetric about the $y$-axis; $x$ and $x^3$ are symmetric about the origin.</p>

<h3>2. Step functions</h3>
<p>A <b>step function</b> has a graph made of horizontal segments. The greatest integer function is the model: on $-2\le x\lt-1$ its value is always $-2$, on $-1\le x\lt0$ it is $-1$, and so on. Each step has a <b>filled</b> dot on its left end (included) and an <b>open</b> dot on its right end (excluded). Calculators in "connected" mode draw misleading vertical segments between the steps; use "dot" mode.</p>
<p><b>Postage</b> is a classic step function: $80$ cents for the first ounce or fraction of an ounce, plus $17$ cents for each additional ounce or fraction. A formula is $P(x)=0.80-0.17\lfloor 1-x\rfloor$. For example $P(2.5)=0.80-0.17\lfloor-1.5\rfloor=0.80-0.17(-2)=1.14$.</p>

<h3>3. Graphs of piecewise-defined functions</h3>
<p>Graph each piece only over its own interval, and mark endpoints carefully.</p>
<div class="lfigs"><div class="lplot" data-f="x*x|-2.4|1|-c;x+2|1|4|oc" data-x="-3,5" data-y="-1,7" data-w="220" data-cap="$f(x)=x^2$ for $x\le1$, $x+2$ for $1\lt x\le4$"></div></div>
<p>At $x=1$ the first piece gives the filled point $(1,1)$; the second piece would give $(1,3)$ but $x=1$ is excluded there, so it's an open circle. The graph has a <b>jump</b> at $x=1$.</p>
<p>The absolute value function is piecewise too: $|x|=x$ for $x\ge0$ and $-x$ for $x\lt0$.</p>

<h3>4. Increasing, decreasing, constant</h3>
<p>$f$ is <b>increasing</b> on an interval if its graph rises as you move left to right there ($a\lt b$ implies $f(a)\lt f(b)$), <b>decreasing</b> if the graph falls, and <b>constant</b> if it is horizontal. Intervals are read along the $x$-axis, never the $y$-axis.</p>
<ul><li>$f(x)=x^2$ decreases on $(-\infty,0)$ and increases on $(0,\infty)$.</li>
<li>$f(x)=|x|+|x-2|$ decreases on $(-\infty,0)$, is constant ($=2$) on $[0,2]$, and increases on $(2,\infty)$. On $[0,2]$, $|x|+|x-2|=x+(2-x)=2$.</li></ul>

<h3>5. Local maxima and minima</h3>
<p>$f$ has a <b>local maximum</b> at $x=c$ if the graph has a peak at $(c,f(c))$: $f(x)\le f(c)$ for all $x$ near $c$. A <b>local minimum</b> is a valley: $f(x)\ge f(d)$ for $x$ near $d$. A peak need not be the highest point of the whole graph, only of its neighborhood.</p>
<ul><li>The <b>location</b> is the $x$-value; the <b>value</b> is the $y$-value. "Local maximum at $x=-1$ with value $4$."</li>
<li>Local extrema are exactly where the graph switches between increasing and decreasing.</li>
<li>Exact locations usually require calculus (or, for parabolas, the vertex formula of Section 4.1). A calculator's maximum/minimum finder approximates them.</li></ul>
<p>Example: $g(x)=0.5x^3-3x$ has a local maximum at $x=-\sqrt2\approx-1.414$ with value $2\sqrt2\approx2.828$ and a local minimum at $x=\sqrt2$ with value $-2\sqrt2$. So $g$ increases on $(-\infty,-\sqrt2)$ and $(\sqrt2,\infty)$ and decreases on $(-\sqrt2,\sqrt2)$.</p>
<div class="lfigs"><div class="lplot" data-f="0.5*x*x*x-3*x" data-x="-4,4" data-y="-5,5" data-w="220" data-pts="-1.4142,2.8284;1.4142,-2.8284" data-cap="$g(x)=0.5x^3-3x$: a peak and a valley"></div></div>
<p><b>Optimization.</b> A $44\times28$ sheet with $x\times x$ squares cut from the corners folds into a box of volume $V(x)=x(44-2x)(28-2x)$, for $0\lt x\lt14$. The maximum finder gives $x\approx5.57$ inches.</p>

<h3>6. The Vertical Line Test</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Vertical Line Test</h4>A graph is the graph of a function exactly when <b>no vertical line meets it more than once</b>.</div>
<p>If a vertical line $x=3$ hit the graph at $(3,2)$ and $(3,-1)$, then the input $3$ would have two outputs, which a function can't do. Circles, sideways parabolas like $x=y^2$, and vertical lines all fail. Beware calculator pictures that seem to fail because of steep, pixelated pieces; zoom in before deciding.</p>

<h3>7. Reading graphs</h3>
<ul><li><b>Solutions of $f(x)=k$:</b> draw the horizontal line $y=k$; each crossing gives one solution.</li>
<li><b>Where $f(x)\gt k$:</b> the $x$-values where the graph is above that line.</li>
<li><b>Composite readings:</b> to find $f(f(2))$, read $f(2)$ from the graph, then read $f$ at that value.</li>
<li>Applied graphs: "in the 1980s" means an interval of the horizontal axis; "the biggest drop" means the steepest downward stretch.</li></ul>

<h3>Worked examples</h3>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 1.</b> How many real solutions does $\lfloor x\rfloor=2x-3$ have?
<details><summary>Solution</summary>The left side is an integer $n$, so $2x-3=n$ and $x=\frac{n+3}{2}$. We also need $n\le x\lt n+1$: $n\le\frac{n+3}2\lt n+1$, i.e. $2n\le n+3$ and $n+3\lt2n+2$, which give $n\le3$ and $n\gt1$. So $n=2$ or $3$, giving $x=2.5$ and $x=3$. <b>Two</b> solutions.</details></div>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 2.</b> Using $P(x)=0.80-0.17\lfloor1-x\rfloor$, find the postage for a $4$-ounce envelope and for a $4.1$-ounce envelope.
<details><summary>Solution</summary>$P(4)=0.80-0.17\lfloor-3\rfloor=0.80+0.51=\$1.31$. $P(4.1)=0.80-0.17\lfloor-3.1\rfloor=0.80+0.68=\$1.48$. Crossing $4$ ounces jumps to the next step.</details></div>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 3.</b> On what interval is $f(x)=x^2-6x+5$ decreasing?
<details><summary>Solution</summary>$f(x)=(x-3)^2-4$ is the parabola $x^2$ moved so its vertex is at $(3,-4)$. It falls until $x=3$ and rises after, so it decreases on $(-\infty,3)$.</details></div>
<div class="warn"><h4>Common traps</h4><ul>
<li>Giving increasing/decreasing intervals in terms of $y$. They are always $x$-intervals.</li>
<li>Confusing where a maximum occurs ($x$) with what the maximum is ($y$).</li>
<li>Ignoring open versus filled endpoints when reading a range: an open circle's height is not attained unless another part of the graph reaches it.</li>
</ul></div>

Q: The postage for a large envelope weighing $x$ ounces is $P(x)=0.80-0.17\lfloor 1-x\rfloor$ dollars. What is the postage for an envelope weighing $3.2$ ounces?
L: 1
A: 1.31
W: 1.14
W: 1.48
W: 1.34
W: 0.97
S: Evaluate the greatest integer part first: $1-3.2=-2.2$, and $\lfloor-2.2\rfloor=-3$ (round <b>down</b> to $-3$, not toward zero to $-2$).
$P(3.2)=0.80-0.17(-3)=0.80+0.51=1.31$ dollars.
This matches the rule in words: $80$ cents for the first ounce plus $17$ cents for each additional ounce or fraction: $3.2$ ounces is the first ounce plus $2.2$ more, which counts as $3$ additional, so $0.80+3(0.17)=1.31$.

Q: What is the minimum value of $f(x)=|x|+|x-2|$, and on what set is it attained?
L: 2
A: 2,\ \text{for every }x\text{ in }[0,2]
W: 0,\ \text{at }x=0
W: 1,\ \text{at }x=1
W: 2,\ \text{only at }x=1
W: 2,\ \text{at }x=0\text{ and }x=2\text{ only}
S: Remove the absolute values interval by interval.
• For $x\lt0$: $f(x)=-x+(2-x)=2-2x$, which is greater than $2$ and decreasing.
• For $0\le x\le2$: $f(x)=x+(2-x)=2$. Constant!
• For $x\gt2$: $f(x)=x+(x-2)=2x-2$, greater than $2$ and increasing.
So the minimum value is $2$, attained at <b>every</b> point of $[0,2]$, where the graph is a horizontal segment.
Interpretation: $|x|+|x-2|$ is the sum of the distances from $x$ to $0$ and to $2$, which is smallest (equal to $2$) anywhere between them.

Q: A box with no top is made from a $44\times28$-inch sheet of cardboard by cutting an $x\times x$ square from each corner and folding up the sides. What is the domain of the volume function $V(x)$ in this situation?
L: 1
A: (0,14)
W: (0,22)
W: (0,28)
W: [0,44]
W: (14,22)
S: The box has height $x$, length $44-2x$, and width $28-2x$, so $V(x)=x(44-2x)(28-2x)$.
For an actual box, every dimension must be positive:
• $x\gt0$,
• $44-2x\gt0\Rightarrow x\lt22$,
• $28-2x\gt0\Rightarrow x\lt14$.
All three hold when $0\lt x\lt14$. (The width is the binding constraint: cut more than $14$ inches from each side of a $28$-inch edge and nothing is left.)

Q: Which equation's graph <b>fails</b> the Vertical Line Test?
L: 1
A: x=y^2-4
W: y=x^2-4
W: y=|x-4|
W: y=\sqrt{x+4}
W: y=x^3-4x
S: The graph fails the test if some vertical line $x=c$ meets it twice, i.e. some $x$ has two $y$-values.
• $y=x^2-4$, $y=|x-4|$, $y=\sqrt{x+4}$, $y=x^3-4x$ are all of the form "$y=$ formula in $x$," so each $x$ gives one $y$. They pass.
• $x=y^2-4$: at $x=0$ we get $y^2=4$, so $y=\pm2$. The points $(0,2)$ and $(0,-2)$ lie on the same vertical line. This sideways parabola <b>fails</b>.

Q: The function $g(x)=\tfrac12x^3-3x$ has a local maximum at $x=-\sqrt2$. What is the local maximum <b>value</b>?
L: 2
A: 2\sqrt2
W: -\sqrt2
W: \sqrt2
W: -2\sqrt2
W: 3\sqrt2
S: The value of a local maximum is the $y$-coordinate: $g(-\sqrt2)$.
$(-\sqrt2)^3=-(\sqrt2)^3=-2\sqrt2$, so $\tfrac12(-\sqrt2)^3=-\sqrt2$.
$-3(-\sqrt2)=3\sqrt2$.
$g(-\sqrt2)=-\sqrt2+3\sqrt2=2\sqrt2\approx2.83$.
($-\sqrt2$ is where the maximum occurs, not its value.)

Q: Let $f(x)=\begin{cases}x^2&x\le1\\x+2&1\lt x\le4\end{cases}$. The graph of $f$ has a jump at $x=1$. What is the vertical distance between the filled endpoint and the open endpoint there?
L: 1
A: 2
W: 1
W: 3
W: 0
W: 4
S: At $x=1$ the first rule applies (because of $\le$), so the filled point is $(1,1^2)=(1,1)$.
The second piece, $y=x+2$, approaches $(1,3)$ as $x$ decreases to $1$, but $x=1$ itself is excluded there, so $(1,3)$ is an open circle.
The gap is $3-1=2$.

Q: How many real numbers $x$ satisfy $\lfloor x\rfloor=2x-3$?
L: 2
A: 2
W: 0
W: 1
W: 3
W: \text{infinitely many}
S: The left side is an integer; call it $n$. Then $2x-3=n$, so $x=\dfrac{n+3}{2}$.
But $\lfloor x\rfloor=n$ also requires $n\le x\lt n+1$:
$n\le\dfrac{n+3}2$ gives $2n\le n+3$, so $n\le3$.
$\dfrac{n+3}2\lt n+1$ gives $n+3\lt2n+2$, so $n\gt1$.
So $n=2$ or $n=3$, giving $x=\frac52$ (check: $\lfloor2.5\rfloor=2=2(2.5)-3$ ✓) and $x=3$ (check: $\lfloor3\rfloor=3=2(3)-3$ ✓).
Exactly $2$ solutions. Graphically, the line $y=2x-3$ crosses the staircase twice.

Q: On which interval is $f(x)=x^2-6x+5$ increasing?
L: 1
A: (3,\infty)
W: (-\infty,3)
W: (-4,\infty)
W: (1,5)
W: (5,\infty)
S: Complete the square: $x^2-6x+5=(x^2-6x+9)-4=(x-3)^2-4$.
The graph is the parabola $y=x^2$ shifted so its vertex is at $(3,-4)$. It opens upward, so it falls to the left of $x=3$ and rises to the right.
Increasing on $(3,\infty)$. (The roots $1$ and $5$ are where it crosses the $x$-axis; they have nothing to do with where it turns.)
---
Q: Let $f(x)=x^3-3x$. What is the sum of its local maximum value and its local minimum value?
L: 2
A: 0
W: 4
W: -4
W: 2
W: -2
S: The local extrema of $x^3-3x$ occur at $x=-1$ and $x=1$ (where the graph turns around).
Local maximum value: $f(-1)=-1+3=2$. Local minimum value: $f(1)=1-3=-2$.
Sum: $2+(-2)=0$.
This is no accident: $f$ is odd ($f(-x)=-f(x)$), so its graph is symmetric about the origin, and the peak at $(-1,2)$ is the mirror image of the valley at $(1,-2)$.
---
Q: What is the range of $f(x)=\lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor -x\rfloor$?
L: 2
A: \{-1,0\}
W: \{0\}
W: \{-1\}
W: \{0,1\}
W: \text{all integers}
S: Split into two cases.
• $x$ an integer: $\lfloor x\rfloor=x$ and $\lfloor-x\rfloor=-x$, so $f(x)=0$.
• $x$ not an integer: write $x=n+t$ with $0\lt t\lt1$. Then $\lfloor x\rfloor=n$ and $-x=-n-t$ lies strictly between $-n-1$ and $-n$, so $\lfloor-x\rfloor=-n-1$. The sum is $-1$.
Both cases occur, so the range is $\{-1,0\}$. The graph is the horizontal line $y=-1$ with isolated points lifted to height $0$ at each integer.
---
Q: A function $f$ is increasing on $(-\infty,-2)$, decreasing on $(-2,5)$, and increasing on $(5,\infty)$. Which statement must be true?
L: 1
A: f\text{ has a local maximum at }x=-2\text{ and a local minimum at }x=5
W: f\text{ has a local minimum at }x=-2\text{ and a local maximum at }x=5
W: f(-2)\text{ is the largest value of }f
W: f(5)\text{ is the smallest value of }f
W: f\text{ has local maxima at both }x=-2\text{ and }x=5
S: A local maximum occurs where the graph switches from rising to falling; a local minimum where it switches from falling to rising.
• At $x=-2$: increasing, then decreasing, so it's a peak: a <b>local maximum</b>.
• At $x=5$: decreasing, then increasing, so it's a valley: a <b>local minimum</b>.
They need not be the overall largest or smallest values: the graph keeps rising after $x=5$ forever, so $f$ eventually exceeds $f(-2)$; and it keeps falling to the left, so values below $f(5)$ occur.
