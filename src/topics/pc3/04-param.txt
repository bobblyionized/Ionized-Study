<h2>3.3.A Parametric Graphing</h2>
<p>Many important curves fail the Vertical Line Test: loops, figure-eights, curves that turn back on themselves. They can't be written as $y=f(x)$, but they can be described as the <b>path of a moving point</b>. If at time $t$ the point is at</p>
$$x=f(t),\qquad y=g(t),$$
<p>these are <b>parametric equations</b> and $t$ is the <b>parameter</b>. The curve is the set of all points $(f(t),g(t))$ as $t$ runs through its allowed values.</p>

<h3>1. Plotting from a table</h3>
<p>For $x=\tfrac12t+1,\ y=t^2$:</p>
<table class="t"><tr><th>$t$</th><td>$-3$</td><td>$-2$</td><td>$-1$</td><td>$0$</td><td>$1$</td><td>$2$</td><td>$3$</td></tr>
<tr><th>$x$</th><td>$-0.5$</td><td>$0$</td><td>$0.5$</td><td>$1$</td><td>$1.5$</td><td>$2$</td><td>$2.5$</td></tr>
<tr><th>$y$</th><td>$9$</td><td>$4$</td><td>$1$</td><td>$0$</td><td>$1$</td><td>$4$</td><td>$9$</td></tr></table>
<p>Plot the points $(x,y)$ in order of increasing $t$ and connect them. The curve is a parabola, traced from left to right. The parameter $t$ itself does not appear on the graph: it only labels <i>when</i> each point is reached.</p>
<div class="lfigs"><div class="lplot" data-f="4*(x-1)*(x-1)|-0.6|2.6|--" data-x="-2,4" data-y="-1,10" data-w="200" data-pts="-0.5,9;0,4;0.5,1;1,0;1.5,1;2,4;2.5,9" data-cap="$x=\tfrac12t+1,\ y=t^2$"></div></div>

<h3>2. Eliminating the parameter</h3>
<p>To find an ordinary equation in $x$ and $y$, solve one equation for $t$ and substitute into the other.</p>
<ul><li>$x=\tfrac12t+1\Rightarrow t=2(x-1)$, so $y=t^2=4(x-1)^2$: the parabola above, vertex $(1,0)$.</li>
<li>$x=2+3t,\ y=-1+4t$: $t=\frac{x-2}{3}$, so $y=-1+\frac43(x-2)=\frac43x-\frac{11}3$: a line of slope $\frac43$. In general, <b>linear</b> parametric equations give a line whose slope is $\dfrac{\text{coefficient of }t\text{ in }y}{\text{coefficient of }t\text{ in }x}$.</li></ul>
<div class="warn"><h4>Watch the restrictions</h4>Eliminating $t$ can add points that the parametric curve never reaches. $x=t^2,\ y=t^4$ gives $y=x^2$, but since $x=t^2\ge0$ the curve is only the <b>right half</b> of that parabola. And $x=3t^2-5,\ y=t^2$ with $-4\le t\le4$ gives $x=3y-5$, but only for $0\le y\le16$: a line <b>segment</b> from $(-5,0)$ to $(43,16)$, traced twice (once for negative $t$, once for positive $t$).</div>

<h3>3. Endpoints, direction, self-crossings</h3>
<ul><li>If $t$ is restricted to $a\le t\le b$, the curve has <b>endpoints</b> $(f(a),g(a))$ and $(f(b),g(b))$. For $x=t^2-t-1,\ y=t^3-4t-6$ with $-2\le t\le3$, the endpoints are $(5,-6)$ and $(5,9)$.</li>
<li>The <b>direction</b> of motion is the direction of increasing $t$. With $x=5-2t$, $x$ decreases as $t$ increases, so the point moves left.</li>
<li>The curve <b>crosses itself</b> where two different parameter values give the same point. For $x=t^3-t,\ y=4-t^2$: $y(t)=y(-t)$ always, and $x(-t)=-x(t)$, so $t$ and $-t$ give the same point exactly when $x=0$, i.e. $t^3-t=0$, $t=\pm1$. The crossing point is $(0,3)$.</li></ul>
<div class="lfigs"><div class="lplot" data-par="t*t*t-t|4-t*t|-2.35|2.35" data-pts="0,3" data-x="-7,7" data-y="-2,5" data-w="240" data-cap="$x=t^3-t,\ y=4-t^2$ crosses itself at $(0,3)$"></div></div>

<h3>4. Graphing $x$ as a function of $y$</h3>
<p>Graphing calculators graph $y$ as a function of $x$. To graph an equation like $x=y^3-3y^2-4y+7$, set $y=t$:</p>
$$x=t^3-3t^2-4t+7,\qquad y=t.$$
<p>The same trick graphs any $y=f(x)$ parametrically: $x=t,\ y=f(t)$.</p>
<p>Sometimes you must solve for $x$ first: $xy^2+xy+x=y^3-2y^2+4$ factors as $x(y^2+y+1)=y^3-2y^2+4$, so $x=\dfrac{t^3-2t^2+4}{t^2+t+1},\ y=t$.</p>

<h3>Worked examples</h3>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 1.</b> Eliminate the parameter: $x=t-3,\ y=t^2+1$.
<details><summary>Solution</summary>$t=x+3$, so $y=(x+3)^2+1$: the parabola $y=x^2$ shifted $3$ left and $1$ up. No restriction is needed because $x=t-3$ takes every real value.</details></div>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 2.</b> For $x=1-t^2,\ y=t^3-t-1$ with $-4\le t\le4$, what $x$-values does the curve reach?
<details><summary>Solution</summary>$t^2$ ranges over $[0,16]$, so $x=1-t^2$ ranges over $[-15,1]$. The largest $x$ is $1$ (at $t=0$); the smallest is $-15$ (at $t=\pm4$).</details></div>
<div class="tip"><h4>Strategy</h4>To understand a parametric curve without a calculator: (1) make a short table, (2) eliminate $t$ if possible, (3) check the restrictions on $x$ and $y$ that come from the original equations, and (4) note the direction as $t$ increases.</div>

Q: The curve $x=t^3-t,\ y=4-t^2$ crosses itself at exactly one point. Which point?
L: 2
A: (0,\,3)
W: (0,\,4)
W: (0,\,0)
W: (3,\,0)
W: (6,\,0)
S: A self-crossing means two different values $t\ne s$ give the same point.
Equal $y$-values: $4-t^2=4-s^2$ means $s=-t$ (since $s\ne t$).
Equal $x$-values with $s=-t$: $x(-t)=-t^3+t=-(t^3-t)$, so we need $t^3-t=-(t^3-t)$, i.e. $t^3-t=0$: $t(t-1)(t+1)=0$.
$t=0$ gives $s=0=t$ (not two different values). $t=\pm1$ works: both give $x=0$, $y=4-1=3$.
The curve passes through $(0,3)$ twice, at $t=-1$ and $t=1$.

Q: Describe the graph of $x=3t^2-5,\ y=t^2$ for $-4\le t\le4$.
L: 2
A: \text{a line segment from }(-5,0)\text{ to }(43,16)
W: \text{the entire line }x=3y-5
W: \text{a parabola opening to the right}
W: \text{a line segment from }(-17,-4)\text{ to }(43,16)

S: Both coordinates depend on $t$ only through $t^2$. Let $u=t^2$; then $x=3u-5$ and $y=u$, so $x=3y-5$: the points lie on a line.

The graph is the segment from $(-5,0)$ to $(43,16)$. (It's traced twice: from $(43,16)$ down to $(-5,0)$ as $t$ goes from $-4$ to $0$, then back up.)

Q: The parametric equations $x=2+3t,\ y=-1+4t$ describe a line. What is its slope?
L: 1
A: \tfrac43
W: \tfrac34
W: 4
W: -\tfrac12
W: \tfrac{11}{3}
S: Solve for $t$ from $x$: $t=\dfrac{x-2}{3}$.
Substitute: $y=-1+4\cdot\dfrac{x-2}{3}=\dfrac43x-\dfrac83-1=\dfrac43x-\dfrac{11}{3}$.
Slope $=\dfrac43$. Shortcut: each unit of $t$ moves $x$ by $3$ and $y$ by $4$, so rise/run $=\frac43$.

Q: What is the graph of $x=t^2,\ y=t^4$ (all real $t$)?
L: 2
A: \text{the right half of }y=x^2\text{, including }(0,0)
W: \text{the whole parabola }y=x^2
W: \text{the left half of }y=x^2
W: \text{the upper half of }x=y^2
W: \text{the line }y=x
S: Eliminate $t$: $y=t^4=(t^2)^2=x^2$.
But $x=t^2$ can never be negative, so only points of $y=x^2$ with $x\ge0$ are reached.
Every $x\ge0$ is reached (take $t=\sqrt x$), so the graph is exactly the right half of the parabola, starting at the origin.
