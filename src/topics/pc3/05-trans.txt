<h2>3.4 Graphs and Transformations</h2>
<p>Most graphs you'll meet are a catalog graph (Section 3.3) that has been <b>moved, stretched, or flipped</b>. If you know how each algebraic change to a formula changes its graph, you can sketch $y=-2\sqrt{x+3}+1$ in seconds from $y=\sqrt x$.</p>

<h3>1. Vertical shifts: change the output</h3>
<div class="box">$y=f(x)+c$ is the graph of $f$ shifted <b>up</b> $c$ units ($c\gt0$), and $y=f(x)-c$ is shifted <b>down</b> $c$ units.</div>
<p>Every output is increased by $c$, so every point $(x,y)$ moves to $(x,y+c)$. Example: $y=x^2+3$ is the parabola raised $3$ units, vertex $(0,3)$.</p>

<h3>2. Horizontal shifts: change the input (opposite direction!)</h3>
<div class="box">$y=f(x+c)$ is the graph of $f$ shifted <b>left</b> $c$ units ($c\gt0$), and $y=f(x-c)$ is shifted <b>right</b> $c$ units.</div>
<p>Why the "wrong" direction? On $y=f(x-3)$, the output that $f$ used to produce at input $0$ is now produced when $x-3=0$, i.e. at $x=3$. Everything happens $3$ units later, which means $3$ units to the right. Point rule: $(x,y)\to(x+3,y)$.</p>
<div class="lfigs">
<div class="lplot" data-f="x*x;(x-3)*(x-3)" data-x="-3,6" data-y="-1,8" data-w="190" data-cap="$y=x^2$ and $y=(x-3)^2$: right $3$"></div>
<div class="lplot" data-f="abs(x);abs(x+2)+1" data-x="-5,4" data-y="-1,7" data-w="190" data-cap="$y=|x|$ and $y=|x+2|+1$: left $2$, up $1$"></div>
</div>

<h3>3. Stretches and compressions</h3>
<div class="box"><ul>
<li>$y=c\,f(x)$ with $c\gt1$: <b>vertical stretch</b> by a factor of $c$ (away from the $x$-axis). With $0\lt c\lt1$: vertical compression. Point rule: $(x,y)\to(x,cy)$.</li>
<li>$y=f(cx)$ with $c\gt1$: <b>horizontal compression</b> by a factor of $\frac1c$ (toward the $y$-axis). With $0\lt c\lt1$: horizontal stretch. Point rule: $(x,y)\to\left(\frac xc,y\right)$.</li></ul></div>
<p>Again the input change acts "backwards": $f(2x)$ reaches each output at <i>half</i> the $x$-value, so the graph is squeezed horizontally. Points on the $x$-axis stay put under vertical stretches; points on the $y$-axis stay put under horizontal ones.</p>

<h3>4. Reflections</h3>
<div class="box"><ul>
<li>$y=-f(x)$: reflection across the <b>$x$-axis</b> (outputs negated): $(x,y)\to(x,-y)$.</li>
<li>$y=f(-x)$: reflection across the <b>$y$-axis</b> (inputs negated): $(x,y)\to(-x,y)$.</li></ul></div>
<div class="lfigs">
<div class="lplot" data-f="sqrt(x)|0|8|c-;-sqrt(x)|0|8|c-" data-x="-8,8" data-y="-4,4" data-w="190" data-cap="$\sqrt x$ and $-\sqrt x$"></div>
<div class="lplot" data-f="sqrt(x)|0|8|c-;sqrt(-x)|-8|0|-c" data-x="-8,8" data-y="-4,4" data-w="190" data-cap="$\sqrt x$ and $\sqrt{-x}$"></div>
</div>

<h3>5. Combining transformations</h3>
<p>Changes to the <b>output</b> (outside $f$) happen in the usual order of operations: multiply (stretch/reflect), then add (shift). For $y=-2\sqrt{x+3}+1$ starting from $\sqrt x$:</p>
<ol><li>$\sqrt{x+3}$: shift left $3$ (starting point $(0,0)\to(-3,0)$).</li>
<li>$2\sqrt{x+3}$: stretch vertically by $2$.</li>
<li>$-2\sqrt{x+3}$: reflect across the $x$-axis.</li>
<li>$-2\sqrt{x+3}+1$: shift up $1$. The starting point ends at $(-3,1)$ and the graph heads down and to the right.</li></ol>
<p>Changes to the <b>input</b> (inside $f$) undo themselves in reverse. To handle $y=f(-x+2)$, factor: $f(-(x-2))$, which is "reflect across the $y$-axis, then shift right $2$." Solve for the original input to map points: if $(5,4)$ is on $f$, then $-x+2=5$ gives $x=-3$, so $(-3,4)$ is on the new graph.</p>
<div class="tip"><h4>The point-mapping method (never fails)</h4>For $y=A\,f(B(x-h))+k$, a point $(a,b)$ on $f$ moves to
$$\left(\frac aB+h,\ Ab+k\right).$$
Inside: solve $B(x-h)=a$ for $x$. Outside: just compute $Ab+k$.</div>

<h3>6. Domains and ranges</h3>
<ul><li>Horizontal transformations change the <b>domain</b>: if $f$ has domain $[-2,6]$, then $g(x)=f(2x-4)$ needs $-2\le2x-4\le6$, so $1\le x\le5$.</li>
<li>Vertical transformations change the <b>range</b>: if $f$ has range $[-3,5]$, then $-2f(x)+1$ has outputs from $-2(5)+1=-9$ to $-2(-3)+1=7$, i.e. $[-9,7]$. A negative factor swaps which end is which.</li></ul>

<h3>Worked examples</h3>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 1.</b> The point $(2,5)$ is on the graph of $f$. What point must be on the graph of $y=3f(x+1)-4$?
<details><summary>Solution</summary>Inside: $x+1=2$ gives $x=1$. Outside: $3(5)-4=11$. The point is $(1,11)$.</details></div>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 2.</b> Write an equation for $y=|x|$ reflected across the $x$-axis and moved so its vertex is at $(-2,3)$.
<details><summary>Solution</summary>Reflect: $y=-|x|$. Move the vertex from $(0,0)$ to $(-2,3)$: left $2$, up $3$: $y=-|x+2|+3$.</details></div>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 3.</b> Describe how $y=(x+1)^3-2$ is obtained from $y=x^3$.
<details><summary>Solution</summary>$x\to x+1$ inside: left $1$. $-2$ outside: down $2$. The center of symmetry moves from $(0,0)$ to $(-1,-2)$.</details></div>
<div class="warn"><h4>Common traps</h4><ul>
<li>$f(x-3)$ moves <b>right</b>, not left.</li>
<li>$f(2x)$ <b>compresses</b> horizontally (by $\frac12$); it does not stretch.</li>
<li>$f(2x+6)=f(2(x+3))$ is a shift <b>left 3</b> (not 6) after the compression. Factor out the coefficient of $x$ first.</li>
<li>$-f(x)$ and $f(-x)$ are different reflections.</li>
</ul></div>
</script>

A: [1,\,5]

Q: The function $f$ has range $[-3,5]$. What is the range of $g(x)=-2f(x)+1$?
L: 2
A: [-9,\,7]
W: [-5,\,11]
W: [-7,\,9]
W: [-6,\,10]
W: [7,\,-9]
S: Every output $y$ of $f$ becomes $-2y+1$.
The smallest output of $f$, $-3$, becomes $-2(-3)+1=7$.
The largest output, $5$, becomes $-2(5)+1=-9$.
Multiplying by a negative number reverses the order, so the new outputs run from $-9$ to $7$: the range is $[-9,7]$.

Q: Which sequence of transformations turns the graph of $y=x^3$ into the graph of $y=(x+1)^3-2$?
L: 1
A: \text{left 1, then down 2}
W: \text{right 1, then down 2}
W: \text{left 1, then up 2}
W: \text{left 2, then down 1}
W: \text{right 1, then up 2}
S: Inside the cube, $x$ is replaced by $x+1$: shift <b>left</b> $1$.
Outside, $-2$ is subtracted from every output: shift <b>down</b> $2$.
The point of symmetry $(0,0)$ of $y=x^3$ moves to $(-1,-2)$.

A: (-3,\,1)
W: (3,\,1)
W: (-3,\,-1)
W: (3,\,-2)
W: (-3,\,-2)

Q: The graph of $y=f(x)$ is stretched vertically by a factor of $3$, then shifted $2$ units down. Which equation results?
L: 1
A: y=3f(x)-2
W: y=3\big(f(x)-2\big)
W: y=f(3x)-2
W: y=3f(x-2)
W: y=\tfrac13f(x)-2
S: Do the operations to the <b>output</b> in the order described.
Stretch vertically by $3$: multiply the output, $y=3f(x)$.
Shift down $2$: subtract from the output, $y=3f(x)-2$.
The order matters: $3\big(f(x)-2\big)=3f(x)-6$ would be shifting down $2$ first and then stretching, which moves the graph down $6$ in total.

Q: The graph of $y=x^2$ is compressed horizontally by a factor of $\tfrac13$. Which equation results, and how does it compare with a vertical stretch?
L: 2
A: y=9x^2\text{, the same as a vertical stretch by }9
W: y=\tfrac13x^2\text{, a vertical compression}
W: y=3x^2\text{, the same as a vertical stretch by }3
W: y=\tfrac19x^2\text{, a vertical compression}
W: y=x^2+3\text{, a vertical shift}
S: A horizontal compression by a factor of $\frac13$ replaces $x$ with $3x$: $y=(3x)^2=9x^2$.
So for this particular function, squeezing horizontally by $3$ is the same as stretching vertically by $3^2=9$.
(This happens because $x^2$ is a power function: $(cx)^2=c^2x^2$. It is not true for most functions.)
