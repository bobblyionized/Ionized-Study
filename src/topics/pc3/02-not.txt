<h2>3.2 Functional Notation</h2>
<p>Functional notation is a shorthand language. If a function is named $f$ and $x$ is an input, then $f(x)$ (read "$f$ of $x$") means <b>the output that $f$ produces from the input $x$</b>. The sentence "the distance the rock has fallen after $t$ seconds is $16t^2$ feet" becomes simply $d(t)=16t^2$, and "the tax on $\$25{,}000$ is $\$693.75$" becomes $T(25000)=693.75$.</p>
<div class="box"><h4>The one rule of evaluation</h4>A formula like $f(x)=\sqrt{x^2+1}$ is a set of directions: "square the input, add $1$, take the square root." To find $f(\text{anything})$, <b>replace every $x$ in the formula by that entire thing, in parentheses</b>.
<ul><li>$f(3)=\sqrt{3^2+1}=\sqrt{10}$, $\quad f(-5)=\sqrt{(-5)^2+1}=\sqrt{26}$</li>
<li>$f(a+1)=\sqrt{(a+1)^2+1}=\sqrt{a^2+2a+2}$</li></ul></div>

<h3>1. Evaluating at expressions</h3>
<p>Let $h(x)=\dfrac{x^2+5}{x-1}$. Then</p>
<ul><li>$h(-2)=\dfrac{(-2)^2+5}{-2-1}=\dfrac{9}{-3}=-3$</li>
<li>$h(-a)=\dfrac{(-a)^2+5}{-a-1}=\dfrac{a^2+5}{-a-1}$</li>
<li>$h(r^2+3)=\dfrac{(r^2+3)^2+5}{(r^2+3)-1}=\dfrac{r^4+6r^2+14}{r^2+2}$</li></ul>
<p><b>$f(-x)$ is not $-f(x)$.</b> If $f(x)=x^2+x-2$, then $f(-x)=(-x)^2+(-x)-2=x^2-x-2$, while $-f(x)=-(x^2+x-2)=-x^2-x+2$. The first changes the <i>input</i>; the second negates the <i>output</i>.</p>

<h3>2. Common mistakes</h3>
<div class="warn"><h4>Functional notation is not multiplication</h4>The parentheses in $f(x)$ do not mean "times," so the distributive law does <b>not</b> apply. In general:
<ul><li>$f(a+b)\ne f(a)+f(b)$. With $f(x)=x^2$: $f(3+2)=25$ but $f(3)+f(2)=13$.</li>
<li>$f(a-b)\ne f(a)-f(b)$.</li>
<li>$f(ab)\ne f(a)f(b)$. With $f(x)=x+7$: $f(3\cdot4)=19$ but $f(3)f(4)=110$.</li>
<li>$f(ab)\ne a\,f(b)$ and $f(ab)\ne f(a)\,b$. With $f(x)=x^2+1$: $f(2\cdot3)=37$ but $f(2)\cdot3=15$.</li></ul>
A few special functions do satisfy some of these ($f(x)=3x$ satisfies $f(a+b)=f(a)+f(b)$), but you can never assume it.</div>

<h3>3. The difference quotient</h3>
<p>For a function $f$ and a number $h\ne0$, the <b>difference quotient</b> is</p>
$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.$$
<p>It measures the average rate of change of $f$ between $x$ and $x+h$ (Section 3.6), and it is the starting point of calculus. To compute it, work in three steps:</p>
<ol><li>Find $f(x+h)$ by replacing $x$ with $x+h$, and expand.</li>
<li>Subtract $f(x)$. <b>Everything without an $h$ cancels.</b> If it doesn't, you've made an error.</li>
<li>Factor $h$ out of every remaining term and cancel it with the denominator.</li></ol>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example.</b> Difference quotient of $f(x)=x^2-x+2$.
<details open><summary>Solution</summary>
$f(x+h)=(x+h)^2-(x+h)+2=x^2+2xh+h^2-x-h+2$.<br>
$f(x+h)-f(x)=(x^2+2xh+h^2-x-h+2)-(x^2-x+2)=2xh+h^2-h$.<br>
$\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}=\dfrac{h(2x+h-1)}{h}=2x+h-1.$</details></div>
<div class="box"><h4>Difference quotients worth remembering</h4><ul>
<li>$f(x)=mx+b$: $\;m$ (a line's rate of change is its slope)</li>
<li>$f(x)=ax^2+bx+c$: $\;2ax+ah+b$</li>
<li>$f(x)=x^3$: $\;3x^2+3xh+h^2$</li>
<li>$f(x)=\dfrac1x$: $\;\dfrac{-1}{x(x+h)}$ (combine the fractions over $x(x+h)$ first)</li>
<li>$f(x)=\sqrt x$: $\;\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt x}$ (multiply top and bottom by the conjugate)</li></ul></div>

<h3>4. Domains: the domain convention</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Domain convention</h4>Unless told otherwise, the domain of $f$ is every real number for which the formula produces a real number.</div>
<p>In practice you only have to look out for two things:</p>
<ul><li><b>Division by zero:</b> exclude any $x$ that makes a denominator $0$. The domain of $k(x)=\frac{x^2+5}{x-1}$ is all reals except $1$.</li>
<li><b>Even roots of negatives:</b> require what's under $\sqrt{\ }$ (or $\sqrt[4]{\ }$, ...) to be $\ge0$. The domain of $f(u)=\sqrt{u+2}$ is $u\ge-2$, that is $[-2,\infty)$.</li></ul>
<p>When both occur, combine the conditions. For $g(t)=\dfrac{\sqrt{t-2}}{t-3}$ we need $t\ge2$ <b>and</b> $t\ne3$: the domain is $[2,3)\cup(3,\infty)$. A root in the denominator, as in $\frac{1}{\sqrt{x-4}}$, needs $x-4>0$ (strictly), since the root can't be $0$ either.</p>
<p>Odd roots ($\sqrt[3]{\ }$) and polynomials accept every real number.</p>

<h3>5. Piecewise-defined functions</h3>
<p>A <b>piecewise-defined</b> function uses different formulas on different parts of its domain:</p>
$$f(x)=\begin{cases}2x+3 & \text{if } x\lt 4\\ x^2-1 & \text{if } 4\le x\le10\end{cases}$$
<p>To evaluate, first decide <b>which condition the input satisfies</b>, then use only that formula: $f(-5)=2(-5)+3=-7$ and $f(8)=8^2-1=63$. You can't find $f(k)$ until you know where $k$ lies. Here the rule says nothing for $x\gt10$, so the domain is $(-\infty,10]$.</p>
<p>Tax schedules are piecewise functions. For the Louisiana tax of Section 3.1:</p>
$$T(x)=\begin{cases}0.021x & 0\le x\lt 12{,}500\\ 262.50+0.0345(x-12{,}500) & 12{,}500\le x\lt 25{,}000\\ 693.75+0.048(x-25{,}000) & x\ge 25{,}000\end{cases}$$

<h3>6. Applications: cost, revenue, profit</h3>
<p>If a factory has fixed costs $F$, each item costs $c$ to make, and sells for $p$, then with $x$ items</p>
<ul><li>Revenue $R(x)=px$, cost $C(x)=cx+F$, and profit $P(x)=R(x)-C(x)=(p-c)x-F$.</li>
<li>The <b>break-even point</b> is where $P(x)=0$, that is, $x=\dfrac{F}{p-c}$. If this isn't a whole number, the factory loses money just below it and makes money just above it.</li>
<li>The domain of an applied function is set by the situation: whole numbers of items, up to the factory's capacity.</li></ul>
<p>Example: fixed costs $\$12{,}000$ a week, $\$0.80$ per cup, selling price $\$1.95$. Then $P(x)=1.15x-12000$, so $P(5000)=-6250$ (a loss), $P(14000)=4100$, and the break-even point is $\frac{12000}{1.15}\approx10{,}434.8$ cups.</p>

<h3>More worked examples</h3>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 1.</b> If $f(x)=|3-x|\sqrt{x-3}+7$, find $f(7)-f(4)$.
<details><summary>Solution</summary>$f(7)=|{-4}|\sqrt4+7=4\cdot2+7=15$ and $f(4)=|{-1}|\sqrt1+7=1+7=8$, so the difference is $7$.</details></div>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 2.</b> Find the domain of $g(r)=\sqrt{r-4}+\sqrt r-2$.
<details><summary>Solution</summary>We need $r-4\ge0$ and $r\ge0$. Both hold exactly when $r\ge4$, so the domain is $[4,\infty)$.</details></div>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 3.</b> Compute the difference quotient of $f(x)=\dfrac1x$.
<details><summary>Solution</summary>$f(x+h)-f(x)=\dfrac1{x+h}-\dfrac1x=\dfrac{x-(x+h)}{x(x+h)}=\dfrac{-h}{x(x+h)}$. Dividing by $h$ gives $\dfrac{-1}{x(x+h)}$.</details></div>
<div class="tip"><h4>Habit that prevents most errors</h4>When you substitute, <b>always write the parentheses first</b>, then fill them in: $f(\ \ )=(\ \ )^2-(\ \ )+2$. Most sign errors come from writing $-x+h$ instead of $-(x+h)$.</div>
