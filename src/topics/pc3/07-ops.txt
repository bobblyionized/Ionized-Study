<h2>3.5 Operations on Functions</h2>
<p>Functions can be combined like numbers (added, subtracted, multiplied, divided) and they can also be <b>composed</b>, feeding the output of one into another. Composition is the genuinely new operation, and it's everywhere: a price that depends on demand, which depends on time, makes price a composite function of time.</p>

<h3>1. Sums, differences, products, quotients</h3>
<div class="box">For functions $f$ and $g$:
<ul><li>$(f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x)$, $\quad(f-g)(x)=f(x)-g(x)$, $\quad(fg)(x)=f(x)\,g(x)$</li>
<li>$\left(\dfrac fg\right)(x)=\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}$</li></ul>
The domain of $f+g$, $f-g$, and $fg$ is the set of $x$ in <b>both</b> domains. The domain of $\frac fg$ is the same, minus every $x$ with $g(x)=0$.</div>
<p>Example: $f(x)=\sqrt x$ (domain $x\ge0$) and $g(x)=x-3$ (all reals). Then $(f+g)(x)=\sqrt x+x-3$ on $[0,\infty)$, and $\left(\frac fg\right)(x)=\frac{\sqrt x}{x-3}$ on $[0,3)\cup(3,\infty)$.</p>
<div class="warn"><h4>Find the domain before simplifying</h4>$\sqrt{x+1}\cdot\sqrt{x-1}$ has domain $x\ge1$, because both factors must exist. Simplifying it to $\sqrt{x^2-1}$ would wrongly suggest that $x\le-1$ is allowed too. Likewise $\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}$ is not defined at $x=1$ even though it simplifies to $x+1$.</div>

<h3>2. Composition</h3>
<div class="box">The <b>composite function</b> $g\circ f$ ("$g$ circle $f$" or "$g$ after $f$") is
$$(g\circ f)(x)=g\big(f(x)\big).$$
Apply $f$ first, then apply $g$ to the result. Its domain is every $x$ in the domain of $f$ for which $f(x)$ is in the domain of $g$.</div>
<p>With $f(x)=3x+2$ and $g(x)=x^2-1$:</p>
<ul><li>$(g\circ f)(2)=g(f(2))=g(8)=63$, but $(f\circ g)(2)=f(g(2))=f(3)=11$.</li>
<li>As formulas: $(g\circ f)(x)=(3x+2)^2-1=9x^2+12x+3$ and $(f\circ g)(x)=3(x^2-1)+2=3x^2-1$.</li></ul>
<p><b>Order matters:</b> $g\circ f$ and $f\circ g$ are usually different functions. (Shifts by constants, like $x+3$ and $x-5$, are an exception: they commute.)</p>
<p><b>Domains of composites.</b> If $f(x)=x-4$ and $g(x)=\sqrt x$, then $(g\circ f)(x)=\sqrt{x-4}$ needs $x-4\ge0$: domain $[4,\infty)$. If $f(x)=\frac1x$ and $g(x)=\frac{1}{x-2}$, then $g(f(x))=\frac{1}{\frac1x-2}$ needs $x\ne0$ (for $f$) <b>and</b> $\frac1x\ne2$, i.e. $x\ne\frac12$ (for $g$). Simplifying to $\frac{x}{1-2x}$ hides the restriction $x\ne0$, so find the domain first.</p>

<h3>3. Decomposing functions</h3>
<p>Calculus often requires the reverse: writing a complicated function as a composite of simpler ones. Look for an "inside" expression.</p>
<ul><li>$h(x)=(3x^2+1)^5$: inside $g(x)=3x^2+1$, outside $f(x)=x^5$, so $h=f\circ g$.</li>
<li>$k(x)=\sqrt{x^3+4}$: inside $x^3+4$, outside $\sqrt x$.</li>
<li>$m(x)=\dfrac{1}{(x-7)^2}$: inside $x-7$, outside $\frac1{x^2}$ (or inside $(x-7)^2$, outside $\frac1x$). Decompositions are not unique.</li></ul>

<h3>4. Solving for an unknown piece</h3>
<p>If $f(x)=2x+1$ and $f(g(x))=6x-5$, then $2g(x)+1=6x-5$, so $g(x)=3x-3$. If instead the <i>outer</i> function is unknown, substitute: from $f(x+1)=x^2$, set $u=x+1$ so $x=u-1$ and $f(u)=(u-1)^2$.</p>

<h3>5. Applications</h3>
<p>An oil spill is circular with radius $r(t)=3t$ feet after $t$ minutes. Its area as a function of the radius is $A(r)=\pi r^2$. The area as a function of time is the composite</p>
$$(A\circ r)(t)=A(3t)=\pi(3t)^2=9\pi t^2.$$
<p>After $10$ minutes the area is $900\pi\approx2827$ square feet.</p>

<h3>Worked examples</h3>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 1.</b> If $f(x)=\dfrac{x}{x+1}$, simplify $(f\circ f)(x)$.
<details><summary>Solution</summary>$f(f(x))=\dfrac{\frac{x}{x+1}}{\frac{x}{x+1}+1}=\dfrac{\frac{x}{x+1}}{\frac{2x+1}{x+1}}=\dfrac{x}{2x+1}$ (for $x\ne-1,-\frac12$).</details></div>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 2.</b> Using the table, find $(f\circ g)(1)$ and $(g\circ f)(1)$.
<table class="t"><tr><th>$x$</th><td>1</td><td>2</td><td>3</td><td>4</td></tr><tr><th>$f(x)$</th><td>3</td><td>1</td><td>4</td><td>2</td></tr><tr><th>$g(x)$</th><td>4</td><td>3</td><td>2</td><td>1</td></tr></table>
<details><summary>Solution</summary>$(f\circ g)(1)=f(g(1))=f(4)=2$. $(g\circ f)(1)=g(f(1))=g(3)=2$. Equal here by coincidence; try $x=2$: $f(g(2))=f(3)=4$ but $g(f(2))=g(1)=4$. Try $x=3$: $f(g(3))=f(2)=1$, $g(f(3))=g(4)=1$. (This particular pair happens to commute!)</details></div>
<div class="tip"><h4>Reading $f\circ g$</h4>Read right to left: in $(f\circ g)(x)$, $x$ goes into $g$ first. Writing $f(g(x))$ with the parentheses avoids every ordering mistake.</div>

L: 2
A: [1,\infty)
W: (-\infty,-1]\cup[1,\infty)
W: [-1,\infty)
W: [-1,1]
W: (-\infty,\infty)
S: $h$ is the product of $f(x)=\sqrt{x+1}$ (domain $x\ge-1$) and $g(x)=\sqrt{x-1}$ (domain $x\ge1$).
