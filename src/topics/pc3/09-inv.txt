<h2>3.7 Inverse Functions</h2>
<p>A function takes inputs to outputs. Sometimes we want to go backwards: given the output, recover the input. If a temperature in Celsius is $F=\frac95C+32$ in Fahrenheit, then $C=\frac59(F-32)$ undoes it. The "undo" function is the <b>inverse</b>. Only certain functions can be undone.</p>

<h3>1. One-to-one functions</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Definition</h4>A function $f$ is <b>one-to-one</b> if different inputs always give different outputs: $a\ne b\Rightarrow f(a)\ne f(b)$. Equivalently, $f(a)=f(b)$ forces $a=b$.</div>
<p>$f(x)=x^2$ is <b>not</b> one-to-one, since $f(3)=f(-3)=9$: knowing the output $9$ doesn't tell you the input. $f(x)=x^3$ is one-to-one.</p>
<div class="box"><h4>Horizontal Line Test</h4>A function is one-to-one exactly when <b>no horizontal line crosses its graph more than once</b>.</div>
<p>Compare the Vertical Line Test (is it a function?) with the Horizontal Line Test (is it one-to-one?). Any function that is always increasing or always decreasing passes the Horizontal Line Test.</p>
<div class="lfigs">
<div class="lplot" data-f="x*x-2;2" data-x="-4,4" data-y="-3,6" data-w="180" data-cap="$x^2-2$ fails: $y=2$ hits twice"></div>
<div class="lplot" data-f="0.3*x*x*x+0.5*x" data-x="-4,4" data-y="-5,5" data-w="180" data-cap="always increasing: passes"></div>
</div>

<h3>2. The inverse function</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Definition</h4>If $f$ is one-to-one, its <b>inverse function</b> $f^{-1}$ is defined by
$$f^{-1}(b)=a\quad\text{exactly when}\quad f(a)=b.$$
The domain of $f^{-1}$ is the range of $f$, and the range of $f^{-1}$ is the domain of $f$.</div>
<p><b>Caution:</b> $f^{-1}(x)$ does <b>not</b> mean $\dfrac{1}{f(x)}$. The $-1$ is notation for "inverse," not an exponent.</p>
<p>Shortcut: to find $f^{-1}(5)$ you don't need a formula. Just solve $f(x)=5$. For $f(x)=x^3+2x+1$, $f(1)=4$, so $f^{-1}(4)=1$.</p>

<h3>3. Finding a formula for $f^{-1}$</h3>
<ol><li>Write $y=f(x)$.</li>
<li>Solve this equation for $x$ in terms of $y$.</li>
<li>The result $x=f^{-1}(y)$ is the inverse; rename the variable to write $f^{-1}(x)$.</li></ol>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example.</b> Find the inverse of $f(x)=\dfrac{2x+1}{x-3}$.
<details open><summary>Solution</summary>$y=\dfrac{2x+1}{x-3}\Rightarrow y(x-3)=2x+1\Rightarrow xy-3y=2x+1\Rightarrow xy-2x=3y+1\Rightarrow x(y-2)=3y+1$, so $x=\dfrac{3y+1}{y-2}$. Thus $f^{-1}(x)=\dfrac{3x+1}{x-2}$.</details></div>
<div class="tip"><h4>Pattern for linear fractions</h4>If $f(x)=\dfrac{ax+b}{cx+d}$, then $f^{-1}(x)=\dfrac{dx-b}{-cx+a}$: swap $a$ and $d$ and negate $b$ and $c$.</div>

<h3>4. The Round-Trip Theorem</h3>
<div class="box">$f$ and $g$ are inverses of each other exactly when
$$g(f(x))=x\ \text{ for every }x\text{ in the domain of }f\quad\text{and}\quad f(g(x))=x\ \text{ for every }x\text{ in the domain of }g.$$</div>
<p>Going there and back returns you to where you started. This is how you <b>verify</b> an inverse: $f(x)=\sqrt[3]{x-1}$ and $g(x)=x^3+1$ satisfy $g(f(x))=(x-1)+1=x$ and $f(g(x))=\sqrt[3]{x^3}=x$, so they are inverses.</p>

<h3>5. Graphs of inverse functions</h3>
<p>If $(a,b)$ is on the graph of $f$, then $(b,a)$ is on the graph of $f^{-1}$. Swapping coordinates is the same as <b>reflecting across the line $y=x$</b>.</p>
<div class="lfigs"><div class="lplot" data-f="x*x|0|2.6|c-;sqrt(x)|0|6.5|c-;x" data-x="-1,6.5" data-y="-1,6.5" data-w="220" data-cap="$x^2$ (for $x\ge0$) and its inverse $\sqrt x$, mirror images across $y=x$"></div></div>

<h3>6. Restricting the domain</h3>
<p>A function that isn't one-to-one can be made one-to-one by restricting its domain to a piece where it's always increasing or always decreasing.</p>
<ul><li>$f(x)=x^2$ with domain $x\ge0$ has inverse $\sqrt x$.</li>
<li>$f(x)=x^2-4x=(x-2)^2-4$ is one-to-one on $[2,\infty)$ (right of the vertex), with inverse $f^{-1}(x)=2+\sqrt{x+4}$.</li>
<li>$f(x)=x^2+1$ with $x\ge0$: $y=x^2+1$ gives $x=\sqrt{y-1}$, so $f^{-1}(x)=\sqrt{x-1}$ on $[1,\infty)$.</li></ul>

<h3>More worked examples</h3>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 1.</b> $f(x)=\sqrt{x-2}+1$. What are the domain and range of $f^{-1}$?
<details><summary>Solution</summary>$f$ has domain $[2,\infty)$ and range $[1,\infty)$ (since $\sqrt{x-2}\ge0$). Swap: $f^{-1}$ has domain $[1,\infty)$ and range $[2,\infty)$. In fact $f^{-1}(x)=(x-1)^2+2$ for $x\ge1$.</details></div>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 2.</b> Which functions are their own inverses?
<details><summary>Solution</summary>$f(x)=\frac1x$, $f(x)=-x$, $f(x)=5-x$, and $f(x)=\frac{x+1}{x-1}$ all satisfy $f(f(x))=x$. Their graphs are symmetric about the line $y=x$.</details></div>
<div class="warn"><h4>Common traps</h4><ul>
<li>Writing $f^{-1}(x)=\frac1{f(x)}$.</li>
<li>Forgetting that the inverse of a restricted function inherits a restricted domain (the range of $f$).</li>
<li>Claiming a function that fails the Horizontal Line Test has an inverse.</li>
</ul></div>

Q: What is the inverse of $f(x)=\dfrac{2x+1}{x-3}$?
L: 1
A: f^{-1}(x)=\dfrac{3x+1}{x-2}
W: f^{-1}(x)=\dfrac{x-3}{2x+1}

W: f^{-1}(x)=\dfrac{3x+1}{2-x}

Multiply by $x-3$: $\;y(x-3)=2x+1$, so $xy-3y=2x+1$.
Collect the $x$ terms: $\;xy-2x=3y+1$, so $x(y-2)=3y+1$.
Divide: $\;x=\dfrac{3y+1}{y-2}$. Renaming, $f^{-1}(x)=\dfrac{3x+1}{x-2}$.
Check with a number: $f(4)=\frac{9}{1}=9$ and $f^{-1}(9)=\frac{28}{7}=4$. ✓
($\frac{x-3}{2x+1}$ is $\frac1{f(x)}$, the reciprocal, which is not the inverse.)

L: 1

W: f(x)=x^2+1
W: f(x)=|x-1|
W: f(x)=x^4-x

Q: Let $f(x)=\sqrt{x-2}+1$. What is the domain of $f^{-1}$?
L: 2
A: [1,\infty)
W: [2,\infty)
W: (-\infty,\infty)
W: [0,\infty)
W: [3,\infty)
S: The domain of $f^{-1}$ is the range of $f$.
Since $\sqrt{x-2}$ takes every value $\ge0$ (as $x$ runs over $[2,\infty)$), $f(x)=\sqrt{x-2}+1$ takes every value $\ge1$.
So the range of $f$, and the domain of $f^{-1}$, is $[1,\infty)$.
(The formula is $f^{-1}(x)=(x-1)^2+2$, but only for $x\ge1$. Without the restriction, $(x-1)^2+2$ wouldn't be one-to-one.)

Q: The function $f(x)=x^2-4x$ is one-to-one on which of these domains?
L: 1
A: [2,\infty)
W: [0,\infty)
W: (-\infty,4]
W: [-2,\infty)
W: (-\infty,\infty)
S: Complete the square: $x^2-4x=(x-2)^2-4$. The vertex is at $x=2$.
The parabola decreases on $(-\infty,2]$ and increases on $[2,\infty)$. On either of those it passes the Horizontal Line Test.
$[2,\infty)$ is offered. The other domains contain points on both sides of $x=2$ that share outputs: for example on $[0,\infty)$, $f(0)=f(4)=0$.

Q: Which pair of functions are inverses of each other?
L: 1
A: f(x)=\sqrt[3]{x-1},\ g(x)=x^3+1
W: f(x)=x^3-1,\ g(x)=\sqrt[3]{x-1}
W: f(x)=2x+3,\ g(x)=\tfrac12x-3
W: f(x)=x^2,\ g(x)=\sqrt x\ \text{(all real }x)
W: f(x)=\tfrac{1}{x+1},\ g(x)=x+1
S: Use the Round-Trip Theorem: both $f(g(x))$ and $g(f(x))$ must equal $x$.
• $\sqrt[3]{x-1}$ and $x^3+1$: $g(f(x))=(x-1)+1=x$ and $f(g(x))=\sqrt[3]{x^3}=x$. ✓
• $x^3-1$ and $\sqrt[3]{x-1}$: $f(g(x))=(x-1)-1=x-2$. ✗ (The inverse of $x^3-1$ is $\sqrt[3]{x+1}$.)
• $2x+3$ and $\frac12x-3$: $g(f(x))=x+\frac32-3$. ✗ (Should be $\frac{x-3}{2}$.)
• $x^2$ on all reals isn't one-to-one: $\sqrt{(-2)^2}=2\ne-2$. ✗
• $\frac{1}{x+1}$ and $x+1$: $f(g(x))=\frac1{x+2}$. ✗

Q: Let $f(x)=x^2+1$ with domain $x\ge0$. Which is $f^{-1}(x)$?
L: 1
A: \sqrt{x-1},\ x\ge1
W: \sqrt{x}-1,\ x\ge0
W: \sqrt{x+1},\ x\ge-1
W: \pm\sqrt{x-1},\ x\ge1
W: \dfrac{1}{x^2+1}
S: Solve $y=x^2+1$ for $x$: $x^2=y-1$, so $x=\pm\sqrt{y-1}$.
Because the domain of $f$ is $x\ge0$, take the nonnegative root: $x=\sqrt{y-1}$.
The domain of $f^{-1}$ is the range of $f$, which is $[1,\infty)$.
So $f^{-1}(x)=\sqrt{x-1}$ for $x\ge1$. (The $\pm$ version isn't a function.)

Q: Which function is its own inverse?
L: 2
A: f(x)=\dfrac{x+1}{x-1}
W: f(x)=\dfrac{x+1}{x+2}
W: f(x)=2x
W: f(x)=x^3
W: f(x)=x+1
S: A function is its own inverse when $f(f(x))=x$.
For $f(x)=\frac{x+1}{x-1}$:
$f(f(x))=\dfrac{\frac{x+1}{x-1}+1}{\frac{x+1}{x-1}-1}=\dfrac{\frac{2x}{x-1}}{\frac{2}{x-1}}=x$. ✓
The others: $2(2x)=4x$; $(x^3)^3=x^9$; $(x+1)+1=x+2$; and $\frac{x+1}{x+2}$ composed with itself is $\frac{2x+3}{3x+5}$.
Using the pattern, $\frac{ax+b}{cx+d}$ has inverse $\frac{dx-b}{-cx+a}$; for $\frac{x+1}{x-1}$ that's $\frac{-x-1}{-x+1}=\frac{x+1}{x-1}$, itself.

$f^{-1}(11)=\frac{8}{2}=4$.
$f^{-1}(4)=\frac{1}{2}$.
