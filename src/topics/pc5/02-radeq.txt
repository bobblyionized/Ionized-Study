<h2>5.1.A Radical Equations</h2>
<p>To solve an equation with a square root, we get rid of the root by squaring. Squaring is safe in one direction only, and that's the whole story of this section.</p>

<div class="box"><h4>Power Principle</h4>If both sides of an equation are raised to the same positive integer power, every solution of the original equation is also a solution of the new one. <b>But the new equation may have extra solutions</b> that don't solve the original. These are called <b>extraneous solutions</b>.</div>
<p>Why: $x-2=3$ has only $x=5$, but $(x-2)^2=9$ also has $x=-1$, because squaring forgets the sign. So <b>after squaring, always check every answer in the original equation</b>.</p>

<h3>1. The method</h3>
<ol><li><b>Isolate</b> one radical on one side.</li>
<li><b>Raise both sides</b> to the power that removes it (square for $\sqrt{\ }$, cube for $\sqrt[3]{\ }$).</li>
<li><b>Solve</b> the resulting equation.</li>
<li><b>Check</b> each answer in the original equation and throw out the extraneous ones.</li></ol>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 1.</b> Solve $5+\sqrt{3x-11}=x$.
<details><summary>Solution</summary>Isolate: $\sqrt{3x-11}=x-5$. Square: $3x-11=x^2-10x+25$, so $x^2-13x+36=0=(x-4)(x-9)$.<br>Check $x=9$: $5+\sqrt{16}=9$ ✓. Check $x=4$: $5+\sqrt1=6\ne4$ ✗.<br>Only $x=9$. (At $x=4$ the right side $x-5$ is negative, but a square root can't be negative.)</details></div>
<div class="lfigs"><div class="lplot" data-f="5+sqrt(3*x-11)-x|3.67|15" data-x="-1,15" data-y="-4,4" data-w="230" data-cap="$y=5+\sqrt{3x-11}-x$ crosses at $x=9$ only"></div></div>
<p>Graphing $y=(\text{left side})-(\text{right side})$ is a quick check: its $x$-intercepts are the real solutions.</p>

<h3>2. Two radicals: square twice</h3>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example 2.</b> Solve $\sqrt{2x-3}-\sqrt{x+7}=2$.
<details><summary>Solution</summary>Isolate one radical: $\sqrt{2x-3}=\sqrt{x+7}+2$.<br>Square (the right side is a binomial): $2x-3=(x+7)+4\sqrt{x+7}+4$, so $x-14=4\sqrt{x+7}$.<br>Square again: $x^2-28x+196=16(x+7)$, so $x^2-44x+84=0=(x-2)(x-42)$.<br>Check: $x=2$ gives $1-3=-2$ ✗; $x=42$ gives $9-7=2$ ✓. Only $x=42$.</details></div>
<div class="warn"><b>Squaring a sum:</b> $(\sqrt{x+7}+2)^2=(x+7)+4\sqrt{x+7}+4$, not $(x+7)+4$. The middle term is where the second radical comes from.</div>

<h3>3. Odd roots and rational exponents</h3>
<ul><li>Cubing never creates extraneous solutions (every real number has exactly one cube root), but checking is still good practice: $\sqrt[3]{x^2-1}=2$ gives $x^2-1=8$, so $x=\pm3$.</li>
<li>$x^{2/3}=9$ means $\left(x^{1/3}\right)^2=9$, so $x^{1/3}=\pm3$ and $x=\pm27$.</li>
<li>$x^{3/2}=8$ means $\left(\sqrt x\right)^3=8$, so $\sqrt x=2$ and $x=4$ (here $x\ge0$ is required).</li>
<li><b>Quadratic in form:</b> $x-3\sqrt x-4=0$ is $u^2-3u-4=0$ with $u=\sqrt x$, so $u=4$ or $u=-1$. Since $\sqrt x\ge0$, only $u=4$ works: $x=16$.</li></ul>

<h3>4. Solving formulas</h3>
<p>Isolate the radical, then square. Example: solve the pendulum formula $T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac Lg}$ for $L$: $\frac{T}{2\pi}=\sqrt{\frac Lg}$, so $\frac{T^2}{4\pi^2}=\frac Lg$ and $L=\frac{gT^2}{4\pi^2}$.</p>

<h3>5. Applications</h3>
<p>Distances in the plane involve $\sqrt{x^2+a^2}$ (Pythagorean Theorem), so travel-time and cable-length problems lead to radical equations. Example: a boat is $6$ miles from the nearest point $P$ on a straight shore. A dock is $x$ miles along the shore from $P$, and the straight route from the boat to the dock is $2$ miles longer than $x$. Then $\sqrt{x^2+36}=x+2$; squaring gives $36=4x+4$, so $x=8$ (check: $\sqrt{100}=10=8+2$ ✓).</p>

<div class="warn"><h4>Common traps</h4><ul>
<li>Squaring before isolating the radical: $(\sqrt{a}+b)^2\ne a+b^2$.</li>
<li>Skipping the check. Extraneous solutions are common, especially when one side could be negative.</li>
<li>Writing $\sqrt x=-1$ has a solution. A principal square root is never negative.</li></ul></div>
