<h2>5.1 Radicals and Rational Exponents</h2>
<p>Exponential and logarithmic functions need powers like $2^{1.5}$ or $10^{\sqrt2}$. This section gives meaning to any real exponent, starting with roots.</p>

<h3>1. $n$th roots</h3>
<p>The <b>$n$th roots</b> of $c$ are the solutions of $x^n=c$. Look at where the line $y=c$ meets the graph of $y=x^n$:</p>
<ul><li><b>$n$ odd:</b> exactly one solution for every $c$ (the graph rises from $-\infty$ to $\infty$).</li>
<li><b>$n$ even:</b> two solutions (one positive, one negative) if $c\gt0$; one ($x=0$) if $c=0$; none if $c\lt0$.</li></ul>
<div class="lfigs">
<div class="lplot" data-f="x*x*x" data-x="-3,3" data-y="-4,4" data-w="170" data-cap="$y=x^3$: one root for every $c$"></div>
<div class="lplot" data-f="x*x*x*x;2" data-x="-3,3" data-y="-1,5" data-w="170" data-cap="$y=x^4$ and $y=2$: two roots"></div>
</div>
<div class="box"><h4>Principal $n$th root</h4>$\sqrt[n]{c}=c^{1/n}$ is <b>the</b> solution of $x^n=c$ when $n$ is odd, and the <b>nonnegative</b> solution when $n$ is even and $c\ge0$.</div>
<p>So $\sqrt[3]{-8}=-2$, $81^{1/4}=3$, $\sqrt[5]{32}=2$, but $\sqrt[4]{-16}$ is not a real number.</p>

<h3>2. Rational exponents</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Definition</h4>For $c\gt0$ and $\frac tk$ in lowest terms with $k\gt0$: $c^{t/k}=\left(c^{t}\right)^{1/k}=\left(c^{1/k}\right)^{t}$.</div>
<p>The two forms agree: $4^{3/2}=(4^3)^{1/2}=\sqrt{64}=8$ and $(4^{1/2})^3=2^3=8$. Usually take the root first, since it keeps the numbers small: $256^{3/4}=(\sqrt[4]{256})^3=4^3=64$.</p>
<p>The definition also works for negative $c$ when $k$ is odd: $(-8)^{2/3}=\left(\sqrt[3]{-8}\right)^2=(-2)^2=4$. <b>Reduce the fraction first:</b> $(-1)^{2/6}=(-1)^{1/3}=-1$, but $\sqrt[6]{(-1)^2}=1$ would be wrong.</p>

<h3>3. Exponent laws</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>For $c,d\gt0$ and any rational (in fact, any real) $r,s$</h4><ol>
<li>$c^rc^s=c^{r+s}$</li><li>$\dfrac{c^r}{c^s}=c^{r-s}$</li><li>$(c^r)^s=c^{rs}$</li>
<li>$(cd)^r=c^rd^r$</li><li>$\left(\dfrac cd\right)^r=\dfrac{c^r}{d^r}$</li><li>$c^{-r}=\dfrac1{c^r}$</li></ol></div>
<p>Example: $(8r^{3/4}s^{-3})^{2/3}=8^{2/3}r^{(3/4)(2/3)}s^{-2}=\dfrac{4r^{1/2}}{s^2}$. And $x^{1/2}(x^{3/4}-x^{3/2})=x^{5/4}-x^2$ (add the exponents).</p>
<div class="warn"><b>Caution:</b> the laws cover products and quotients only. $(c+d)^r\ne c^r+d^r$; for example $\sqrt{9+16}=5$, not $3+4$.</div>
<p><b>Factoring with rational exponents.</b> $x^{2/3}+x^{1/3}-6$ is a quadratic in $u=x^{1/3}$: $u^2+u-6=(u+3)(u-2)$, so it equals $(x^{1/3}+3)(x^{1/3}-2)$.</p>

<h3>4. Radical notation and simplifying</h3>
<p>$c^{t/k}=\sqrt[k]{c^t}=\left(\sqrt[k]c\right)^t$, and the laws read $\sqrt[n]{cd}=\sqrt[n]c\,\sqrt[n]d$ and $\sqrt[n]{\frac cd}=\frac{\sqrt[n]c}{\sqrt[n]d}$. To simplify, pull out perfect $n$th powers:</p>
<ul><li>$\sqrt{63}=\sqrt{9\cdot7}=3\sqrt7$.</li>
<li>$\sqrt{12}-\sqrt{75}=2\sqrt3-5\sqrt3=-3\sqrt3$ (only like radicals combine).</li>
<li>$\sqrt[3]{40k^4}=\sqrt[3]{8k^3}\,\sqrt[3]{5k}=2k\sqrt[3]{5k}$.</li></ul>

<h3>5. Rationalizing</h3>
<p>Multiply by $1$ written as a well-chosen fraction:</p>
<ul><li>One radical: $\dfrac7{\sqrt5}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt5}{\sqrt5}=\dfrac{7\sqrt5}5$.</li>
<li>A sum or difference: use the <b>conjugate</b> and $(a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2$. $\dfrac2{3+\sqrt6}\cdot\dfrac{3-\sqrt6}{3-\sqrt6}=\dfrac{2(3-\sqrt6)}{9-6}=\dfrac{6-2\sqrt6}3$.</li>
<li>Cube roots: use $a^3+b^3=(a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2)$. $\dfrac1{\sqrt[3]2+1}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt[3]4-\sqrt[3]2+1}{\sqrt[3]4-\sqrt[3]2+1}=\dfrac{\sqrt[3]4-\sqrt[3]2+1}{3}$.</li>
<li><b>Rationalizing the numerator</b> of a difference quotient (used in calculus): $\dfrac{\sqrt{x+h}-\sqrt x}{h}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt x}{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt x}=\dfrac{(x+h)-x}{h(\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt x)}=\dfrac1{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt x}$. Now you can see it approaches $\frac1{2\sqrt x}$ as $h\to0$.</li></ul>

<h3>6. Irrational exponents</h3>
<p>$10^{\sqrt2}$ is the number that $10^{1.4},10^{1.41},10^{1.414},\dots$ approach (about $25.9545$). So $a^t$ makes sense for every real $t$ when $a\gt0$, and all the exponent laws still hold.</p>

<div class="warn"><h4>Common traps</h4><ul>
<li>Applying exponents to sums: $(x+y)^{1/2}\ne x^{1/2}+y^{1/2}$.</li>
<li>Not reducing $\frac tk$ before using a negative base.</li>
<li>A negative exponent means reciprocal, not negative: $27^{-2/3}=\frac19$.</li></ul></div>
