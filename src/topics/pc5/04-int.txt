<h2>5.2.A Compound Interest and the Number $e$</h2>
<p>Interest that earns interest is the clearest example of exponential growth, and it is also where the number $e$ comes from.</p>

<div class="box"><h4>Compound Interest Formula</h4>If $P$ dollars is invested at interest rate $r$ <b>per time period</b> (as a decimal), the amount after $t$ periods is
$$A=P(1+r)^t.$$</div>
<p><b>Why:</b> each period adds $r$ times the current balance, which multiplies the balance by $1+r$.</p>

<h3>1. Reading financial language</h3>
<p>"$6.4\%$ compounded quarterly" means the <b>annual</b> rate is $6.4\%$, split into $4$ periods per year: the rate per period is $\frac{0.064}4=0.016$, and $3.5$ years is $3.5\cdot4=14$ periods.</p>
<div class="box"><h4>Translating</h4>Annual rate $R$ compounded $n$ times per year for $y$ years: $\;r=\dfrac Rn$ and $t=ny$, so $A=P\left(1+\dfrac Rn\right)^{ny}$.</div>
<p>$\$3500$ for $3.5$ years at $6.4\%$:</p>
<ul><li>annually: $3500(1.064)^{3.5}\approx\$4348.74$</li>
<li>quarterly: $3500(1.016)^{14}\approx\$4370.99$</li>
<li>monthly: $3500\left(1+\frac{0.064}{12}\right)^{42}\approx\$4376.14$</li></ul>
<p>The more often interest is compounded, the more you earn, but the gains get smaller and smaller.</p>
<p><b>Careful with periods:</b> at $18\%$ per <i>week</i>, a $\$50$ loan grows to $50(1.18)^{52}\approx\$273{,}422.58$ in $52$ weeks. (A $365$-day year is actually $\frac{365}7$ weeks.)</p>

<h3>2. Solving for time, rate, or present value</h3>
<ul><li><b>Doubling time:</b> $5000\left(1+\frac{0.065}{12}\right)^t=10000$ means $\left(1+\frac{0.065}{12}\right)^t=2$. With technology (or the logarithms of Section 5.5), $t\approx128.3$ months $\approx10.7$ years. Notice $P$ doesn't matter for doubling.</li>
<li><b>Rate:</b> what annual rate turns $\$16{,}000$ into $\$50{,}000$ in $18$ years? $(1+r)^{18}=\frac{50000}{16000}=3.125$, so $1+r=3.125^{1/18}$ and $r\approx0.0653$, about $6.53\%$.</li>
<li><b>Present value:</b> the amount to invest <i>now</i> to have $A$ later is $P=\dfrac{A}{(1+r)^t}$. To have $\$10{,}000$ in $5$ years at $6\%$ compounded annually, invest $\frac{10000}{1.06^5}\approx\$7472.58$.</li></ul>

<h3>3. The number $e$</h3>
<p>Invest $\$1$ for a year at $100\%$ interest, compounded $n$ times: $A=\left(1+\frac1n\right)^n$.</p>
<table class="exmix"><tr><th>$n$</th><th>$\left(1+\frac1n\right)^n$</th></tr>
<tr><td>$1$ (annually)</td><td>$2$</td></tr><tr><td>$4$ (quarterly)</td><td>$2.4414$</td></tr><tr><td>$12$ (monthly)</td><td>$2.6130$</td></tr>
<tr><td>$365$ (daily)</td><td>$2.7146$</td></tr><tr><td>$1000$</td><td>$2.7169$</td></tr><tr><td>$1{,}000{,}000$</td><td>$2.71828$</td></tr></table>
<p>The values increase, but they level off at a limit: the number $e\approx2.718281828$. No matter how often you compound, $\$1$ can't grow past about $\$2.72$.</p>
<div class="lfigs"><div class="lplot" data-f="pow(1+1/x,x)|0.2|60;2.718281828" data-x="0,60" data-y="1,3" data-w="240" data-cap="$(1+\frac1x)^x$ approaches $y=e$"></div></div>

<div class="box"><h4>Continuous Compounding</h4>If $P$ dollars is invested at annual rate $r$, compounded continuously, the amount after $t$ years is
$$A=Pe^{rt}.$$</div>
<p><b>Why:</b> with $n$ compoundings, $P\left(1+\frac rn\right)^{nt}=P\left[\left(1+\frac rn\right)^{n/r}\right]^{rt}$, and the bracket approaches $e$ as $n$ grows (it's $(1+\frac1m)^m$ with $m=\frac nr$).</p>
<p>Example: $\$3800$ at $3.8\%$ compounded continuously for $7.5$ years: $3800e^{0.285}\approx\$5053.10$. Reaching $\$5000$ takes $t$ with $3800e^{0.038t}=5000$, about $7.22$ years.</p>

<div class="warn"><h4>Common traps</h4><ul>
<li>Using the annual rate for each period: monthly compounding at $6\%$ uses $0.005$ per period, not $0.06$.</li>
<li>Counting periods in years: $5$ years compounded quarterly is $t=20$ periods.</li>
<li>Writing the rate as a percent: $3.8\%$ is $r=0.038$.</li></ul></div>
