<h2>5.4 Properties of Logarithms</h2>
<p>Logarithms are exponents, so the exponent laws turn into logarithm laws. These laws let you expand or combine logarithms, and they're how logarithms turn multiplication into addition.</p>

<div class="box"><h4>Logarithm Laws</h4>For all $v,w\gt0$ and any real $k$:
<ul><li><b>Product Law:</b> $\ln(vw)=\ln v+\ln w$</li>
<li><b>Quotient Law:</b> $\ln\left(\dfrac vw\right)=\ln v-\ln w$</li>
<li><b>Power Law:</b> $\ln(v^k)=k\ln v$</li></ul>
The same laws hold for $\log$ (and, in 5.4.A, for every base).</div>

<h3>1. Why they're true</h3>
<p><b>Product Law.</b> By the inverse property, $v=e^{\ln v}$ and $w=e^{\ln w}$. So $vw=e^{\ln v}e^{\ln w}=e^{\ln v+\ln w}$ (add exponents). Taking $\ln$ of both sides: $\ln(vw)=\ln v+\ln w$.</p>
<p><b>Power Law.</b> $v^k=\left(e^{\ln v}\right)^k=e^{k\ln v}$ (multiply exponents), so $\ln(v^k)=k\ln v$.</p>
<p><b>Quotient Law.</b> $\ln\frac vw=\ln(v\cdot w^{-1})=\ln v+\ln w^{-1}=\ln v-\ln w$, using the other two laws.</p>

<h3>2. Expanding and combining</h3>
<ul><li><b>Expand:</b> $\ln\dfrac{x^2\sqrt y}{z}=\ln x^2+\ln y^{1/2}-\ln z=2\ln x+\tfrac12\ln y-\ln z$.</li>
<li><b>Combine into one logarithm:</b> $\ln x+3\ln y-\ln z=\ln x+\ln y^3-\ln z=\ln\dfrac{xy^3}{z}$. Move coefficients up as exponents <i>first</i> (Power Law), then combine.</li>
<li><b>Exact values:</b> $\log5+\log20=\log100=2$, and $\log8-\log2=\log4$.</li>
<li><b>In terms of $u=\ln x$ and $v=\ln y$:</b> $\ln(x^3y^2)=3u+2v$ and $\ln\dfrac{\sqrt x}{y}=\tfrac12u-v$.</li>
<li><b>From known values:</b> with $\ln2\approx0.693$ and $\ln3\approx1.099$: $\ln12=\ln(2^2\cdot3)=2\ln2+\ln3\approx2.485$.</li></ul>

<div class="warn"><h4>What the laws do NOT say</h4><ul>
<li>$\ln(v+w)\ne\ln v+\ln w$. There is no law for the log of a sum or difference.</li>
<li>$\ln v\cdot\ln w\ne\ln(vw)$, and $\dfrac{\ln v}{\ln w}\ne\ln\dfrac vw$.</li>
<li>$(\ln x)^2\ne2\ln x$; only $\ln(x^2)=2\ln x$ (for $x\gt0$).</li></ul>
A quick numeric check exposes these: $\ln(1+1)=\ln2\approx0.693$, but $\ln1+\ln1=0$.</div>

<h3>3. Application: the Richter scale</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Earthquake magnitude</h4>An earthquake of intensity $i$ has Richter magnitude $R(i)=\log\dfrac{i}{i_0}$, where $i_0$ is a fixed reference (minimum) intensity.</div>
<p>By the Product Law, $R(10i)=\log10+\log\frac{i}{i_0}=1+R(i)$: <b>multiplying the intensity by $10$ adds $1$ to the magnitude.</b> In general, multiplying the intensity by $10^k$ adds $k$.</p>
<p>So two earthquakes whose magnitudes differ by $d$ differ in intensity by a factor of $10^d$. The 1906 San Francisco earthquake ($8.3$) was $10^{8.3-7.1}=10^{1.2}\approx16$ times as intense as a $7.1$ earthquake. An earthquake $1000=10^3$ times as intense as a magnitude-$5.0$ quake has magnitude $8.0$.</p>

Q: Which statement is <b>false</b> for all positive $v$ and $w$?

($\sqrt[3]{x^2+1}\ne\sqrt[3]{x^2}+1$.)
