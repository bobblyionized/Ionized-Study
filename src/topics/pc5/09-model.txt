<h2>5.6 Exponential, Logarithmic, and Other Models</h2>
<p>Given real data, which kind of function fits it best? This section is about recognizing the shape of the data and checking it with a quick computation, before letting a calculator's regression find the exact model.</p>

<h3>1. The menu of models</h3>
<table class="exmix"><tr><th>Model</th><th>Form</th><th>Shape and tell-tale sign</th></tr>
<tr><td>Linear</td><td>$y=ax+b$</td><td>constant differences between successive $y$-values</td></tr>
<tr><td>Quadratic</td><td>$y=ax^2+bx+c$</td><td>constant second differences; one turn</td></tr>
<tr><td>Power</td><td>$y=ax^r$</td><td>passes through the origin; $(\ln x,\ln y)$ is linear</td></tr>
<tr><td>Exponential</td><td>$y=ab^x$</td><td>constant <b>ratios</b> of successive $y$-values (equal $x$-steps)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Logarithmic</td><td>$y=a+b\ln x$</td><td>rises ever more slowly; $(\ln x,y)$ is linear</td></tr>
<tr><td>Logistic</td><td>$y=\dfrac{c}{1+ae^{-kx}}$</td><td>S-shaped: fast growth that levels off at $c$</td></tr></table>

<h3>2. Checking for an exponential model: ratios</h3>
<p>If $y=ab^x$, then going from $x$ to $x+1$ multiplies $y$ by $b$: $\dfrac{ab^{x+1}}{ab^x}=b$. So for equally spaced $x$-values, <b>constant ratios of successive $y$-values</b> mean exponential growth or decay.</p>
<p>Example: $100,\ 115,\ 132.25,\ 152.09$ has ratios $1.15$ each time, so $y=100(1.15)^x$. Compare: $3,\ 6,\ 12,\ 24$ (ratio $2$) is exponential, but $3,\ 6,\ 9,\ 12$ (difference $3$) is linear.</p>
<p><b>Two points determine an exponential model.</b> Through $(0,50)$ and $(3,400)$: $a=50$ and $50b^3=400$, so $b^3=8$, $b=2$: $y=50\cdot2^x$.</p>

<h3>3. Power and logarithmic models: take logs</h3>
<ul><li><b>Power:</b> if $y=ax^r$, then $\ln y=\ln a+r\ln x$, which is linear in $\ln x$. So if the points $(\ln x,\ln y)$ lie on a line with slope $r$, a power model fits, with exponent $r$.</li>
<li><b>Logarithmic:</b> $y=a+b\ln x$ is linear in $\ln x$. A sign: multiplying $x$ by a constant adds a constant to $y$. For example $x=1,10,100,1000$ and $y=2,5,8,11$ fit $y=2+3\log x$.</li>
<li><b>Exponential</b> again: $\ln y=\ln a+x\ln b$ is linear in $x$, so $(x,\ln y)$ is linear.</li></ul>

<h3>4. Logistic models</h3>
<p>$y=\dfrac c{1+ae^{-kx}}$ ($k\gt0$) starts near $\frac c{1+a}$, grows fastest in the middle, and levels off at the upper limit $c$. Populations with limited resources and the spread of news or a disease often look like this.</p>
<p>When does $y=\dfrac{1000}{1+9e^{-0.5x}}$ reach half its limit? $\frac{1000}{1+9e^{-0.5x}}=500$ means $1+9e^{-0.5x}=2$, so $e^{-0.5x}=\frac19$ and $x=2\ln9\approx4.39$.</p>

<h3>5. Using a model</h3>
<p>Once you have a model, plug in: $y=4.2(1.08)^x$ predicts $4.2(1.08)^{10}\approx9.07$ at $x=10$; $y=5x^{2.7}$ gives $5\cdot2^{2.7}\approx32.5$ at $x=2$. Be careful far outside the data: an exponential model predicts unlimited growth, while a logistic model levels off. Choose the one that makes sense for the situation.</p>
<div class="warn"><h4>Common traps</h4><ul>
<li>Checking differences when you should check ratios (or the other way around).</li>
<li>Using ratios with unequally spaced $x$-values.</li>
<li>Trusting a model far beyond the data it was fit to.</li></ul></div>

W: \text{linear}

W: \text{linear}
W: \text{power}
W: \text{quadratic}

W: y=115^x

Constant ratio $1.15$ and starting value $100$ give $y=100(1.15)^x$ ($15\%$ growth per step).

Q: For a data set, the points $(\ln x,\ln y)$ lie on a straight line with slope $2.7$. What kind of model fits the original data?
L: 2
A: y=ax^{2.7}
W: y=a(2.7)^x
W: y=a+2.7\ln x
W: y=2.7x+a
W: y=ae^{2.7x}
S: For a power model $y=ax^r$: $\ln y=\ln a+r\ln x$. That's a line in the variables $(\ln x,\ln y)$ with slope $r$.
So slope $2.7$ means $y=ax^{2.7}$.

Q: Data: when $x=1,10,100,1000$, $y=2,5,8,11$. Which model fits exactly?
L: 2
A: y=2+3\log x
W: y=2\cdot3^x
W: y=2+3x
W: y=2x^3
W: y=2+\log 3x
S: Each time $x$ is multiplied by $10$, $y$ goes up by $3$. That's the sign of a logarithmic model.
Try $y=a+b\log x$: at $x=1$, $\log1=0$ gives $a=2$. At $x=10$: $2+b=5$, so $b=3$. Check $x=1000$: $2+3\cdot3=11$ ✓.

Q: The logistic model $y=\dfrac{1000}{1+9e^{-0.5x}}$ levels off at $1000$. At what $x$ does it reach $500$?
L: 3
A: 2\ln9\approx4.39
W: \frac{\ln9}{2}\approx1.10
W: \ln9\approx2.20
W: 9
W: 18
S: Set $\dfrac{1000}{1+9e^{-0.5x}}=500$: then $1+9e^{-0.5x}=2$, so $e^{-0.5x}=\frac19$.
Take $\ln$: $-0.5x=\ln\frac19=-\ln9$, so $x=2\ln9\approx4.39$.

Q: A power model is $y=5x^{2.7}$. What is $y$ when $x=2$?

A: \approx32.5
W: 27
W: \approx10.8
W: \approx65.0
W: \approx6.5
S: $y=5\cdot2^{2.7}\approx5(6.498)\approx32.5$.

Q: An exponential model $y=ab^x$ passes through $(0,50)$ and $(3,400)$. What is it?
L: 2
A: y=50\cdot2^x
W: y=50\cdot8^x
W: y=50+\frac{350}{3}x
W: y=400\cdot2^x
W: y=50\cdot3^x
S: At $x=0$: $y=a=50$. At $x=3$: $50b^3=400$, so $b^3=8$ and $b=2$.
The model is $y=50\cdot2^x$.

Q: For equally spaced $x$-values, the second differences of the $y$-values are all equal (but not zero). Which model fits?
L: 1
A: \text{quadratic}
W: \text{linear}
W: \text{exponential}
W: \text{logarithmic}
W: \text{power with exponent }\tfrac12
S: Constant first differences mean linear; constant <b>second</b> differences mean quadratic $y=ax^2+bx+c$ (just as in Section 4.4.A).
---
Q: A model of the form $y=a+b\ln x$ passes through $(1,4)$ and $(e,7)$. What is it?
L: 2
A: y=4+3\ln x
W: y=7+4\ln x
W: y=4+7\ln x
W: y=4e^{3x}
W: y=3+4\ln x
S: At $x=1$: $\ln1=0$, so $a=4$. At $x=e$: $\ln e=1$, so $4+b=7$ and $b=3$.
Model: $y=4+3\ln x$.
---
Q: Which model predicts that the quantity keeps increasing forever, but never exceeds a fixed limit?
L: 1
A: \text{logistic } y=\frac{c}{1+ae^{-kx}},\ k\gt0
W: \text{exponential } y=ab^x,\ b\gt1
W: \text{logarithmic } y=a+b\ln x,\ b\gt0
W: \text{power } y=ax^r,\ r\gt0
W: \text{quadratic } y=ax^2+bx+c,\ a\gt0
S: A logistic model with $k\gt0$ always increases and has the horizontal asymptote $y=c$, so it never passes $c$.
The logarithmic, exponential, power, and (eventually) quadratic models all grow without any upper limit, even if, like $\ln x$, very slowly.
