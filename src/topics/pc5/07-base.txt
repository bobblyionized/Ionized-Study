<h2>5.4.A Logarithmic Functions to Other Bases</h2>
<p>Bases $10$ and $e$ are the ones on your calculator, but any positive base $b\ne1$ works the same way.</p>

<div class="box"><h4>Definition</h4>For $b\gt0$, $b\ne1$, and $v\gt0$: $\;\log_b v=u\;$ means $\;b^u=v$.<br>So $\log_b v$ is the exponent you put on $b$ to get $v$. $\log_{10}=\log$ and $\log_e=\ln$.</div>
<ul><li>$\log_2 32=5$ because $2^5=32$.</li>
<li>$\log_3\frac1{81}=-4$ because $3^{-4}=\frac1{81}$.</li>
<li>$\log_5\sqrt5=\frac12$ because $5^{1/2}=\sqrt5$.</li>
<li>$\log_4 8=\frac32$ because $4^{3/2}=(\sqrt4)^3=8$. (Tip: write both as powers of $2$: $4^u=8$ means $2^{2u}=2^3$, so $u=\frac32$.)</li></ul>
<p><b>Translating</b> works exactly as before: $2^{-3}=\frac18$ is the same statement as $\log_2\frac18=-3$.</p>

<h3>1. Properties</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Properties of $\log_b$</h4><ul>
<li>$\log_b1=0$ and $\log_bb=1$.</li>
<li>$\log_b(b^u)=u$ for every $u$, and $b^{\log_b v}=v$ for every $v\gt0$. ($\log_bx$ and $b^x$ are inverse functions.)</li>
<li>The domain of $\log_bx$ is $x\gt0$; its graph passes through $(1,0)$ and $(b,1)$ and has vertical asymptote $x=0$. It rises when $b\gt1$ and falls when $0\lt b\lt1$.</li></ul></div>
<div class="lfigs"><div class="lplot" data-f="log(x)/log(2)|0.03|9;log(x)/log(0.5)|0.03|9" data-x="-1,9" data-y="-4,4" data-w="220" data-cap="$\log_2x$ (rising) and $\log_{1/2}x$ (falling)"></div></div>

<h3>2. Logarithm Laws (any base)</h3>
<div class="box">For $v,w\gt0$ and any $k$: $\;\log_b(vw)=\log_bv+\log_bw$, $\;\log_b\frac vw=\log_bv-\log_bw$, $\;\log_b(v^k)=k\log_bv$.</div>
<p>Same proofs as 5.4, with $b$ in place of $e$. Examples: $\log_2 12-\log_2 3=\log_2 4=2$; $\;2\log_5x-\log_5y+\log_53=\log_5\dfrac{3x^2}{y}$.</p>
<p><b>Solving for the unknown:</b></p>
<ul><li>$\log_3x=4$: $x=3^4=81$.</li>
<li>$\log_x64=3$: $x^3=64$, so $x=4$.</li>
<li>$\log_2(x+1)=5$: $x+1=32$, so $x=31$.</li></ul>

<h3>3. Change of base</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Change of Base Formula</h4>For any $b\gt0$, $b\ne1$, and $v\gt0$: $$\log_bv=\frac{\ln v}{\ln b}=\frac{\log v}{\log b}.$$</div>
<p><b>Proof.</b> Let $u=\log_bv$, so $b^u=v$. Take $\ln$ of both sides and use the Power Law: $u\ln b=\ln v$, so $u=\frac{\ln v}{\ln b}$.</p>
<p>This is how to compute any logarithm on a calculator: $\log_7 19=\frac{\ln19}{\ln7}\approx\frac{2.9444}{1.9459}\approx1.5131$. Check: $7^{1.5131}\approx19$ ✓.</p>
<p>It also shows every log function is a constant multiple of $\ln x$: $\log_bx=\frac1{\ln b}\ln x$. For example $\log_9x=\frac{\ln x}{\ln9}=\frac{\ln x}{2\ln3}=\frac12\log_3x$.</p>

<div class="warn"><h4>Common traps</h4><ul>
<li>$\dfrac{\ln v}{\ln b}$ is not $\ln\dfrac vb$ and not $\ln v-\ln b$.</li>
<li>Getting the formula upside down: the base goes on the bottom.</li>
<li>Bases must be positive and not $1$; inputs must be positive.</li></ul></div>
