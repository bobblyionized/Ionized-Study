<h2>Kublai Khan and the Yuan Dynasty</h2>
<p class="meta">Textbook pp. 325–329 · Module 9, Lesson 2 (continued)</p>
<div class="box"><h4>Big idea</h4>Genghis Khan’s grandson <b>Kublai Khan</b> conquered all of China in 1279, the first time a foreigner ruled the whole country, and founded the <b>Yuan Dynasty</b> (1279–1368). Mongol rule opened China to trade and visitors like Marco Polo, but it kept the Chinese out of power, and resentment, costly wars, and disasters brought it down.</div>
<div class="tip"><b>Unit questions this lesson answers:</b> How might the Chinese have felt about their lack of power in Kublai Khan’s government? What factors contributed to the rise and fall of the Yuan Dynasty?<br><b>Key terms:</b> Kublai Khan, Yuan Dynasty, Marco Polo</div>

<h3>Kublai Khan becomes emperor</h3>
<ul><li><b>Kublai Khan</b> (1215–1294), grandson of Genghis Khan, became <b>Great Khan in 1260</b>. In theory he ruled the whole Mongol Empire; in reality it had split into four khanates, so he focused on his own: Mongolia, Korea, Tibet, and northern China.</li>
<li>First he had to finish his grandfather’s goal of conquering <b>all</b> of China. The Song held him off for years, but his armies won in <b>1279</b>.</li>
<li>China had often lost land to northern nomads, but <b>no foreigner had ever ruled the whole country</b> until now.</li></ul>

<h3>A new dynasty: the Yuan</h3>
<p>Kublai founded the <b>Yuan Dynasty</b>. It lasted less than a century (until 1368) but mattered for three reasons:</p>
<ol><li>Kublai <b>united China for the first time in more than 300 years</b>, so he is considered one of China’s great emperors.</li>
<li>Mongol control across Asia <b>opened China to more foreign contacts and trade</b>.</li>
<li>Kublai and his successors <b>tolerated Chinese culture</b> and made <b>few changes to the system of government</b>.</li></ol>
<p>Unlike his ancestors, Kublai left the steppe and enjoyed the luxurious life of a Chinese emperor, with a summer palace at <b>Shangdu</b> and a new square-walled capital at the site of modern <b>Beijing</b>. Moving the capital from Mongolia to China showed he meant to be emperor of China. Still, he stayed a Mongol warrior at heart: he dressed as a Chinese emperor and supported Chinese artists, yet planted steppe grass in his Beijing gardens and loved to hunt.</p>

<h3>Failure to conquer Japan</h3>
<ul><li>Kublai sent huge fleets against Japan in <b>1274</b> and <b>1281</b>. The Mongols forced the <b>Koreans</b> to build, sail, and supply the ships, which nearly ruined Korea.</li>
<li>The second fleet carried <b>150,000</b> Mongol, Chinese, and Korean warriors, the largest seaborne invasion in history until World War II. After 53 days of fighting, a <b>typhoon</b> wrecked the Mongol ships.</li>
<li>The Japanese called it the <b>kamikaze</b>, or “divine wind,” that saved Japan. Both invasions failed.</li></ul>

<h3>Mongol rule in China</h3>
<p>A Chinese adviser told Kublai that an empire can be conquered on horseback but not <b>governed</b> on horseback. Mongol ways would not work for a civilization as complex as China, and there were few Mongols compared with the huge Chinese population, so Kublai needed non-Mongol officials.</p>
<div class="warn"><h4>Mongols and Chinese kept apart</h4><ul>
<li>Mongols lived <b>separately</b> from the Chinese and obeyed <b>different laws</b>.</li>
<li>The Chinese were <b>kept out of high government offices</b>, though many Chinese officials kept local jobs.</li>
<li>Most top posts went to <b>Mongols or foreigners</b>, whom the Mongols trusted more because they had no local loyalties.</li></ul></div>
<p>Kublai was still an able leader. He <b>restored the Grand Canal</b> and extended it 135 miles north to Beijing, and built a paved highway of about 1,100 miles from Hangzhou to Beijing, so the north had a steady supply of grain from the south.</p>

<h3>Foreign trade and Marco Polo</h3>
<ul><li>Thanks to the <b>Mongol Peace</b>, caravan routes across Central Asia were safe, and foreign trade grew. Chinese silk and porcelain went west, along with Chinese inventions: <b>printing, gunpowder, the compass, paper money, and playing cards</b>.</li>
<li>Kublai invited foreign merchants, mostly Muslims from India, Central Asia, and Persia. European traders and Christian missionaries arrived too.</li>
<li><b>Marco Polo</b> (1254–1324), a young trader from <b>Venice</b>, traveled the Silk Roads with his father and uncle and reached Kublai’s court around <b>1275</b>. He learned several Asian languages, and Kublai sent him on government missions for <b>17 years</b>. The Polos left in 1292.</li>
<li>Later, imprisoned during a war between Venice and Genoa, Polo told his stories to a fellow prisoner, who turned them into a book. It described China’s cities and wealth, the burning of “black stones” (coal), Kublai’s government, and Beijing’s trade. It was an instant success, but most readers thought it was tall tales.</li>
<li>People still debate whether he really went: Chinese records don’t mention him, and his book leaves out tea, acupuncture, and foot binding. On his deathbed he said he had told barely half of what he saw.</li></ul>

<h3>The end of Mongol rule</h3>
<ul><li>Late in his reign, Kublai sent costly, failed expeditions into <b>Southeast Asia</b>. Spending on wars, public works, and the luxuries of the court drained the treasury and made the <b>overtaxed Chinese</b> resentful.</li>
<li>Kublai died in <b>1294</b>. His successors were less able and fought over the throne: four khans ruled in one eight-year period.</li>
<li>In the 1300s rebellions broke out. The Chinese had long resented their Mongol rulers, and the humiliation got worse under Kublai’s successors. <b>Famine, floods, disease, economic problems, and corruption</b> fed the revolts.</li>
<li>In <b>1368</b> Chinese rebels overthrew the Mongols, and their leader founded the <b>Ming Dynasty</b> (lasted until 1644).</li>
<li>The rest of the Mongol Empire also fell apart: the Ilkhanate in Persia in the 1330s, the Chagatai khans in the 1370s; only the Golden Horde lasted, ruling Russia for 250 years until Ivan III won independence in 1480. Korea kept its own dynasty but had to accept harsh Mongol terms.</li></ul>

<div class="box"><h4>Answering the unit questions</h4><ul>
<li><b>How might the Chinese have felt about their lack of power?</b> Resentful and humiliated: they lived under foreign rulers who kept them out of high office, made them obey different laws, gave top jobs to foreigners, and overtaxed them. That resentment fueled the rebellions that ended the Yuan.</li>
<li><b>Rise of the Yuan:</b> Mongol military power (Kublai finished conquering the Song in 1279), plus Kublai’s choice to rule as a Chinese-style emperor from Beijing and to keep the Chinese system of government.</li>
<li><b>Fall of the Yuan:</b> costly failed wars (Japan, Southeast Asia), heavy spending and taxes, weak successors fighting over the throne, Chinese resentment, and famine, floods, disease, and corruption.</li></ul></div>
