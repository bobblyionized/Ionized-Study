<h2>Rise of the Mongols</h2>
<p class="meta">Textbook pp. 320–325, stopping at “Kublai Khan Becomes Emperor” · Module 9, Lesson 2</p>
<div class="box"><h4>Big idea</h4>The Mongols, nomads from the steppe, conquered settled societies across much of Asia and built the <b>largest unified land empire in world history</b>. Their rule also helped spread Chinese ideas to the West by encouraging trade.</div>
<div class="tip"><b>Unit questions this lesson answers:</b> Who were the Mongols, and how did they build the largest land empire in world history? What military tactics did Genghis Khan use in war?<br><b>Key terms:</b> pastoralist, clan, Genghis Khan, Pax Mongolica</div>

<h3>Nomads of the Asian steppe</h3>
<p>The <b>steppe</b> is a vast belt of dry grassland across Eurasia. It mattered to its neighbors in two ways: it was a <b>land trade route between East and West</b>, and it was home to <b>nomads who often raided, looted, and conquered</b> the settled lands around it.</p>
<ul><li>The <b>western steppe</b> runs from Central Asia to eastern Europe (home of invaders like the Hittites). The <b>eastern steppe</b>, present-day Mongolia, was the first home of the Huns, the Turks, and the Mongols.</li>
<li>Little rain falls, but short, hardy grasses grow. Temperatures in Mongolia swing from about −57°F in winter to 96°F in summer. The west is milder, so peoples historically moved <b>west and south</b>.</li></ul>
<p><b>The nomadic way of life.</b> Nomads were <b>pastoralists</b>: they herded domesticated animals. They moved constantly to find pasture, but they did not wander; they followed a seasonal pattern back to the same campsites, and groups fought over grass and water rights.</p>
<ul><li>They practically lived on horseback and depended on their animals for everything: meat and mare’s milk, clothing of skins and wool, and portable felt tents called <b>yurts</b>.</li>
<li>They traveled in <b>clans</b>: kinship groups whose members claimed a common ancestor. Clans joined together when they needed a big force to attack an enemy or raid settled neighbors.</li></ul>
<p><b>Nomads and settled peoples.</b> Often they traded peacefully: nomads swapped horses for grain, metal, cloth, and tea. But nomads, proud of their toughness, were sometimes tempted by the wealth of towns and took what they wanted. A <b>strong, organized</b> state could protect its frontier; a <b>weak, divided</b> one suffered more raids. Sometimes nomads conquered a whole empire, and over generations the conquerors became part of the civilization they ruled.</p>

<h3>Genghis Khan unites the Mongols</h3>
<ul><li>The Mongols had roamed the eastern steppe in loosely organized clans. Around 1200 a clan leader named <b>Temujin</b> set out to unite them, defeating his rivals one by one.</li>
<li>When Temujin was about nine, the rival Tatars poisoned his father, and his family lived in deep poverty. He later took revenge on the Tatars. Driven by revenge and a love of conquest, he once said a man’s greatest fortune was to defeat his enemies and seize all they had.</li>
<li>In <b>1206</b> he accepted the title <b>Genghis Khan</b>, “<b>universal ruler</b>” of the Mongol clans. Over the next 21 years he conquered much of Asia.</li>
<li>His first goal was China; he invaded the northern <b>Jin</b> Empire in 1211. Then, after Muslims murdered Mongol traders and an ambassador, he launched a campaign of terror across Central Asia, destroying cities like Utrar, Samarkand, and Bukhara. By <b>1225</b> Central Asia was under Mongol control.</li></ul>

<h3>Genghis the conqueror: his tactics</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Three keys to his success</h4><ol>
<li><b>A brilliant organizer.</b> Following the Chinese military’s model, he grouped warriors into armies of <b>10,000</b>, divided into 1,000-man brigades, 100-man companies, and 10-man squads, each led by his most loyal, battle-proven men.</li>
<li><b>A gifted strategist.</b> He used tricks to confuse enemies. In the <b>fake retreat</b>, a small cavalry unit attacked and then pretended to flee; when the enemy chased it, the rest of the Mongol army appeared and destroyed them.</li>
<li><b>Cruelty as a weapon.</b> He terrified enemies into surrendering: a city that refused to open its gates might have its whole population killed. Fear spread ahead of his armies, and many towns gave up without a fight.</li></ol></div>
<p><b>A mighty fighting force.</b> Mongol soldiers were superb horsemen who trained in yearly game hunts. Each rode with <b>three extra horses</b>, so he could stay in the saddle for up to ten days and cover as much as <b>120 miles a day</b>; if food ran out, he might drink his horse’s blood. The <b>stirrup</b>, invented on the steppe, let a rider stand, turn, and shoot arrows behind him. Warriors carried leather armor, a lance, a dagger, and a bow and arrows, and wore silk underwear that arrows often did not pierce. The army moved like a city, with families, livestock, and thousands of horses; <b>women</b> ran the camp, milked the animals, treated the wounded, and some fought. When attacking, warriors left the caravan and struck from several directions at once. All of this made the army <b>self-sufficient</b>.</p>

<h3>The Mongol Empire</h3>
<ul><li>Genghis Khan died of illness in <b>1227</b>. His sons and grandsons kept conquering: in less than 50 years the Mongols took territory from <b>China to Poland</b>, the largest unified land empire in history.</li>
<li>They finished conquering northern China, invaded Korea, leveled the Russian city of Kiev, and reached the Adriatic Sea, with Venice and Vienna in reach. In the 1250s they stopped moving west and turned to Persia.</li>
<li>By <b>1260</b> the empire was split into four <b>khanates</b>, each ruled by a descendant of Genghis:</li></ul>
<table class="exmix"><tr><th>Khanate</th><th>Region</th></tr>
<tr><td>Khanate of the Great Khan</td><td>Mongolia and China (also Korea)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Khanate of Chagatai</td><td>Central Asia</td></tr>
<tr><td>Ilkhanate</td><td>Persia</td></tr>
<tr><td>Khanate of the Golden Horde</td><td>Russia</td></tr></table>
<p><b>The Mongols as rulers.</b> Many areas they invaded never recovered: some cities’ populations were wiped out, and destroyed irrigation systems (as in the Tigris and Euphrates valleys) left land that could not support people again. Yet the Mongols were <b>tolerant in peace</b>: they rarely forced their beliefs or way of life on others, and some adopted local cultures (the Ilkhans and the Golden Horde became Muslim). These growing differences helped split the empire apart.</p>

<h3>The Mongol Peace</h3>
<p>From the mid-1200s to the mid-1300s, the Mongols kept stability, law, and order across much of Eurasia: the <b>Pax Mongolica</b>, or Mongol Peace. They guaranteed safe passage for caravans, travelers, and missionaries across the whole empire.</p>
<ul><li>Trade between Europe and Asia had never been more active, and ideas and inventions traveled with the goods: Chinese innovations like <b>gunpowder</b> reached Europe.</li>
<li>Some historians think the Mongols also spread the <b>bubonic plague</b> that devastated Europe in the 1300s, along the trade routes or through infected troops.</li></ul>

<div class="box"><h4>Answering the unit questions</h4><ul>
<li><b>Who were the Mongols?</b> Nomadic pastoralists from the eastern steppe (Mongolia) who lived on horseback in clans.</li>
<li><b>How did they build the largest land empire?</b> Genghis Khan united the clans (1206); superb horsemanship, organization, clever tactics, and terror let them conquer Central Asia and northern China; his heirs pushed from China to Poland and ruled through four khanates.</li>
<li><b>Genghis Khan’s tactics:</b> units of 10, 100, 1,000, and 10,000 under loyal commanders; tricks like the fake retreat; terror that made cities surrender without a fight; fast, self-sufficient cavalry with spare horses.</li></ul></div>
