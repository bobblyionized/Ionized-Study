<h2>Han Emperors in China</h2>
<p class="meta">Textbook pp. 256–265 · Module 7, Lesson 3</p>
<div class="box"><h4>Big idea</h4>The Han Dynasty (202 BC–AD 220) expanded China’s borders and built a system of government, a centralized bureaucracy run by Confucian officials chosen by exam, that lasted for centuries. A strong central government has been part of Chinese life ever since.</div>
<div class="tip"><b>Class goal:</b> identify the Han Dynasty’s role in unifying China.<br><b>Key terms:</b> Han Dynasty, centralized government, civil service, monopoly, assimilation</div>

<h3>The Han restore unity</h3>
<p>After Shi Huangdi died in 210 BC, his son was a weak, ineffective leader and the government fell apart. Peasants were bitter over high taxes, harsh labor quotas, and severe punishments, and they rebelled. Rival kings raised armies to win back their old lands.</p>
<p><b>Liu Bang founds the Han.</b> Two leaders emerged from the civil war. <b>Xiang Yu</b>, an aristocratic general, would let warlords keep their territories if they accepted him as their feudal lord. <b>Liu Bang</b> was one of Xiang Yu’s generals. Liu Bang turned against him, won their final battle in <b>202 BC</b>, and declared himself the first emperor of the <b>Han Dynasty</b>.</p>
<ul><li>The Han ruled for more than 400 years in two periods: the <b>Former Han</b> (about two centuries, until AD 9) and, after a short break, the <b>Later Han</b> (almost two more centuries).</li>
<li>The Han shaped China so much that many Chinese still call themselves “people of the Han.”</li></ul>
<div class="box"><h4>Liu Bang’s policies</h4><ul>
<li><b>Internal:</b> destroyed the rival kings’ power and kept Shi Huangdi’s <b>centralized government</b>, in which a central authority controls the running of the state. Hundreds of local provinces called <b>commanderies</b> reported to him.</li>
<li><b>Winning support:</b> he dropped Shi Huangdi’s strict Legalism, <b>lowered taxes</b>, and <b>softened harsh punishments</b>. People appreciated the peace and stability he brought.</li></ul></div>
<p><b>Empress Lü.</b> When Liu Bang died in 195 BC, his son became emperor in name only; his mother, <b>Empress Lü</b>, really ruled. After her son died she kept control by naming infants as emperor. When she died in 180 BC, Liu Bang’s loyal supporters returned to power and executed her relatives. Palace plots like this happened throughout the Han: wives and their families competed to have their sons chosen as heir, and these power struggles sometimes kept the government from working well.</p>

<h3>Wudi, the “Martial Emperor”</h3>
<p><b>Wudi</b>, Liu Bang’s great-grandson, reigned from <b>141 to 87 BC</b>, longer than any other Han emperor. He continued centralizing and is called the <b>Martial (warlike) Emperor</b> because he expanded China through war.</p>
<ul><li><b>The Xiongnu</b> were fierce nomads, deadly archers on horseback, who raided China’s farmland from the northern and western steppes. Earlier Han emperors paid them off with silk, rice, alcohol, and money, but the raids continued. Seeing that the bribes only made them stronger, Wudi sent more than <b>100,000 soldiers</b> against them, drove them into Central Asia, and settled troops on their old pastures.</li>
<li>He allied with the <b>Yuezhi</b>, enemies of the Xiongnu (the Xiongnu had killed the Yuezhi king). Historian Sima Qian records that Wudi sent an envoy to make the alliance: <b>the enemy of his enemy was a useful friend</b>.</li>
<li>He colonized Manchuria and Korea in the northeast and conquered south into present-day Vietnam. By the end of his reign the empire nearly reached the borders of present-day China.</li></ul>

<h3>A highly structured society</h3>
<p>The Chinese believed the emperor had <b>divine authority</b>: he linked heaven and earth. If he ruled well, China had peace and prosperity; if not, heaven showed its anger with earthquakes, floods, and famines.</p>
<table class="exmix"><tr><th>Han social order, top to bottom</th></tr>
<tr><td><b>Emperor</b>: considered semidivine</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Kings and governors</b>, appointed by the emperor, helped by state officials, nobles, and scholars</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Peasant farmers</b>: their food was vital to the empire</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Artisans and merchants</b></td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Soldiers</b>, who guarded the frontiers</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Enslaved persons</b>, usually conquered peoples</td></tr></table>
<p><b>Structures of government.</b> The emperor relied on a complex <b>bureaucracy</b>. Each <b>commandery</b> (province) had a governor who collected taxes, recruited laborers, and handed out justice. Peasants paid part of their crops in taxes, merchants paid taxes too, and peasants owed <b>a month of labor or military service every year</b>, which built roads, canals, and irrigation ditches, filled the armies, and expanded the Great Wall.</p>

<h3>Confucianism, the road to success</h3>
<ul><li>Wudi’s government employed more than <b>130,000 people</b> in 18 ranks of <b>civil service</b> jobs: government jobs won by passing <b>examinations</b>.</li>
<li>Wudi favored Confucian scholars because Confucius taught exactly the qualities he wanted in officials: respect, generosity, truthfulness, <b>diligence</b> (hard work), and kindness. He set up a school where job seekers studied Confucius.</li>
<li>Exams covered <b>history, law, literature, and Confucianism</b>. In theory anyone could take them; in practice only sons of <b>wealthy landowners</b> could afford the education. Even so, the system worked so well that it lasted until <b>1912</b>.</li>
<li>Confucian values of the Han: the family is central to the well-being of the state; the father has complete authority; rulers govern <b>by example, not force</b>; government should educate the people as well as feed and protect them.</li>
<li>Confucian ideas spread through the commanderies to Korea and Vietnam, and later to Japan.</li></ul>

<h3>Han technology, commerce, and culture</h3>
<ul><li><b>Paper</b> was invented in <b>AD 105</b>. It was cheaper than silk, so books and education spread; it was easier to keep records on than strips of wood, so the bureaucracy grew. (A Han official, Ts’ai Lun, made strong paper from mulberry bark, rags, and hemp; papermaking later spread to Korea, Japan, the Islamic world, and Europe.)</li>
<li>The <b>collar harness</b> let horses pull much heavier loads than European harnesses. The Han also perfected a <b>two-bladed plow</b>, improved <b>iron tools</b>, invented the <b>wheelbarrow</b>, and used <b>water mills</b> to grind grain. These helped feed a population that grew to <b>60 million</b>.</li>
<li><b>Agriculture versus commerce:</b> with so many to feed, farming was the most honored job and trade the least honored, yet trade and manufacturing mattered a lot. The government set up <b>monopolies</b> (exclusive control over making and selling a good) on <b>salt, iron, coins, and alcohol</b>, and ran silk mills. Silk-making became a <b>state secret</b>, and silk traveled the <b>Silk Roads</b> across Asia and, through India, to Rome.</li></ul>

<h3>The Han unify Chinese culture</h3>
<p><b>Assimilation</b> means making conquered peoples part of Chinese culture. The government sent Chinese farmers to settle new areas, encouraged them to marry local people, and set up schools to teach local people Confucianism, then appointed local scholars to government jobs.</p>
<ul><li><b>Sima Qian</b> (145–85 BC), the Grand Historian, wrote <i>Records of the Grand Historian</i>, a history from ancient times to Wudi, by visiting historic sites, interviewing eyewitnesses, and studying records and artifacts.</li>
<li>The <i>History of the Former Han Dynasty</i> was started by Ban Biao and finished by his son Ban Gu and daughter <b>Ban Zhao</b>, who also wrote <i>Lessons for Women</i> (be humble and obedient, but also hardworking).</li></ul>
<p><b>Women’s roles.</b> Following Confucian teaching, most women lived quiet lives devoted to their families, working in the home and the family’s fields. Some upper-class women lived differently: a few empresses held great power, Daoist and later Buddhist nuns could be educated, and some women ran shops or practiced medicine.</p>

<h3>The fall of the Han and their return</h3>
<ul><li><b>The rich take advantage of the poor.</b> A father’s land was divided equally among his sons, so plots shrank each generation. Small farmers fell into debt to large landowners, who charged high interest and took the land when farmers could not pay. Large landowners paid no taxes, so the government squeezed the small farmers harder. The gap between rich and poor grew.</li>
<li><b>Political instability.</b> Court advisers, servants, and powerful families plotted over who would be emperor; from about 32 BC to AD 9, one inexperienced emperor followed another, and peasant revolts spread.</li>
<li><b>Wang Mang</b>, a Confucian scholar acting as regent for an infant emperor, took the throne in <b>AD 9</b>, ending the Former Han. He minted new money, opened public granaries, and took land from the rich to give to the poor. This angered powerful landholders, and his extra money led merchants to raise prices.</li>
<li>In AD 11 a great flood left millions homeless; the granaries could not feed them. Peasant revolts broke out, the wealthy joined them, and rebels killed Wang Mang in <b>AD 23</b>. Within two years the Han family took back the throne: the <b>Later Han</b>.</li>
<li>The Later Han regained Silk Road posts and was prosperous at first, but the same economic imbalance, palace plots, and unrest returned. By <b>220</b> the Han had broken into three rival kingdoms.</li></ul>

<div class="box"><h4>The Han’s role in unifying China</h4><ul>
<li><b>Politically:</b> restored unity after the Qin, kept a centralized government with commanderies, and expanded nearly to today’s borders.</li>
<li><b>Through government:</b> a Confucian bureaucracy chosen by civil service exam that lasted until 1912.</li>
<li><b>Culturally:</b> assimilation, a common written Chinese language across the empire, shared Confucian values, and histories that gave China a shared past. That is why Chinese call themselves “people of the Han.”</li></ul></div>
