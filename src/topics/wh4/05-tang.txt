<h2>Tang and Song China</h2>
<p class="meta">Textbook pp. 310–319 · Module 9, Lesson 1 (class reading pp. 310–317)</p>
<div class="box"><h4>Big idea</h4>Under the Tang (618–907) and Song (960–1279), China had a long <b>golden age</b>: it became the richest, most powerful, and most advanced country in the world. Inventions from this time, like printing, gunpowder, and the compass, changed history.</div>
<div class="tip"><b>Unit questions this lesson answers:</b> What were the major achievements of China’s Golden Age? How did the civil service exam lead to a bureaucracy?<br><b>Key terms:</b> Grand Canal, Tang Taizong, Wu Zhao, Song Dynasty, movable type, gentry</div>

<h3>Setting the stage: the Sui reunite China</h3>
<p>After the Han collapsed in AD 220, no emperor could hold China together; over 350 years more than 30 local dynasties rose and fell. By 589 an emperor named <b>Wendi</b> united north and south again and restored a strong central government as the first emperor of the <b>Sui Dynasty</b> (581–618).</p>
<ul><li>The Sui’s greatest accomplishment was the <b>Grand Canal</b>, a waterway of more than 1,000 miles connecting the <b>Huang He</b> (Yellow River) and the <b>Chang Jiang</b> (Yangtze). It became a vital trade route between the northern cities and the rice-growing south.</li>
<li>About a million peasants worked five years to dig it, and perhaps half died. Thousands more died rebuilding the Great Wall.</li>
<li>Overworked and overtaxed, the people revolted, and in 618 the second Sui emperor was assassinated.</li></ul>

<h3>The Tang Dynasty expands China (618–907)</h3>
<p><b>Tang Taizong</b> (reigned 626–649) began the Tang’s great achievements. Born Li Shimin, a distinguished general, he took the throne by killing his brothers and forcing his father, the first Tang emperor, to step aside. Taizong means “Great Ancestor.”</p>
<ul><li>His armies won back the northern and western lands lost since the Han and pushed China’s borders to Manchuria, Vietnam, and the Aral Sea.</li>
<li>With gifted advisers he reformed the government and the law code, which became models for all of East Asia.</li></ul>
<p><b>Wu Zhao</b> (625–705) came to Taizong’s court at 13 as one of his secondary wives. After he died she became the favored wife of his son, then empress. From about 660 she held the real power while weak emperors sat on the throne, and by 668 China’s influence reached Korea. In <b>690</b> she took the title of emperor herself, the <b>only woman ever to do so in China</b>. A strong leader, she kept building and expanding China until she lost power at 80.</p>
<p>Tang rulers strengthened the central government: they expanded the roads and canals begun by the Sui, which pulled the empire together, and promoted foreign trade and better farming.</p>

<h3>Scholar-officials: how the exam built a bureaucracy</h3>
<ul><li>To manage their huge empire, the Tang needed a large bureaucracy. They <b>revived and expanded the civil service exam begun by the Han</b>.</li>
<li>The few who passed the tough exams became an elite group of <b>scholar-officials</b>.</li>
<li>In theory any man could take the exams, but only the wealthy could afford the education, and men with connections sometimes got high posts without testing.</li>
<li>Still, the system created an intelligent, capable governing class. Before the Tang a few noble families dominated; now <b>talent and education mattered more than noble birth</b>, and many moderately wealthy families shared in governing China.</li></ul>

<h3>The Tang lose power</h3>
<ul><li>In the mid-700s, crushing taxes caused hardship but still did not cover the cost of wars and building.</li>
<li>In <b>751</b> Muslim armies defeated the Chinese at the <b>Battle of Talas</b>, and Central Asia slipped out of Chinese control.</li>
<li>Border attacks and rebellions wore the government down. In <b>907</b> rebels burned the capital, Ch’ang-an, and killed the last Tang emperor, a child.</li></ul>

<h3>The Song Dynasty restores China (960–1279)</h3>
<p>After the Tang fell, warlords split China. In <b>960</b> a general named <b>Taizu</b> reunited it as the first Song emperor. The Song ruled a <b>smaller</b> empire than the Han or Tang, but China stayed stable, powerful, and prosperous.</p>
<ul><li>The Song never won back the western lands lost after 751 or the northern lands lost to nomads. They tried paying their northern enemies tribute in silver, silk, and tea, but it failed.</li>
<li>In the early 1100s the <b>Jurchen</b>, a people from Manchuria, conquered the north (the Jin Empire) and forced the Song south across the Huang He. After <b>1127</b> the Song ruled only southern China: the <b>Southern Song</b> (1127–1279), with a grand new capital at <b>Hangzhou</b>.</li>
<li>Even so, the south boomed and became China’s economic heartland; merchants grew rich trading with northern China, Central Asia, western Asia, and Europe.</li></ul>

<h3>An era of prosperity and innovation</h3>
<p>China’s population nearly doubled to about <b>100 million</b>; by the Song there were at least ten cities of a million people. China was the most populous and most advanced country in the world.</p>
<table class="exmix"><tr><th>Achievement</th><th>What it was</th></tr>
<tr><td><b>Movable type</b></td><td>Individual character blocks arranged in a frame to print a page, instead of carving a whole page into one block</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Gunpowder</b></td><td>Led in time to bombs, grenades, small rockets, and cannons</td></tr>
<tr><td>Porcelain, the mechanical clock, <b>paper money</b></td><td>New goods and tools for trade and daily life</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Magnetic compass</b> for sailing</td><td>Made long sea voyages and ocean trade possible</td></tr>
<tr><td>Mathematics</td><td>Advances in arithmetic and algebra; ideas like negative numbers spread south and west</td></tr>
<tr><td>Medicine</td><td>Anatomy charts and models improved acupuncture</td></tr></table>
<p><b>Agriculture.</b> Around 1000, China imported a <b>fast-ripening rice from Vietnam</b> that allowed <b>two harvests a year</b>; officials handed out seedlings across the country. Farmers also greatly expanded <b>terraced fields</b> (level platforms cut into hillsides), opening hilly land to farming. More food fed the growing cities.</p>
<p><b>Trade and foreign contacts.</b> Tang armies guarded the Silk Roads; after China lost them, merchants turned to <b>ocean trade</b>, helped by the compass. China’s ports were the largest in the world, sending ships to Korea, Japan, India, the Persian Gulf, and Africa. Arab traders lived in Chinese cities. <b>Buddhism</b> spread from China to Vietnam, Korea, and Japan, while Islam and some Eastern Christian groups came into China.</p>
<p><b>Revival of Confucianism.</b> After the Han fell, Buddhism had become China’s most important belief system. Late Tang and Song scholars reinterpreted Confucian texts for new questions, a movement called <b>Neo-Confucianism</b>. Because the Song exam required mastering the classics, it spread Confucian beliefs, which then reached Korea, Japan, and Vietnam.</p>
<p><b>Poetry and art.</b> The Tang produced great poets: <b>Li Bo</b> wrote about life’s pleasures; <b>Tu Fu</b> praised order and Confucian virtues and wrote about the hardships of war. Song painters, influenced by <b>Daoism</b>, painted natural landscapes and single branches or flowers, mostly in black ink.</p>

<h3>Changes in Chinese society</h3>
<ul><li>People moved to cities, and there was more <b>social mobility</b> than ever. The civil service was the main way to rise.</li>
<li>The old aristocratic families faded. A new, larger upper class of scholar-officials and their families, the <b>gentry</b>, won their status through <b>education and civil service posts</b> rather than land.</li>
<li>Below them was an <b>urban middle class</b> of merchants, shopkeepers, skilled artisans, and minor officials; then laborers, soldiers, and servants. The <b>peasants</b> in the countryside were by far the largest class.</li>
<li><b>Women’s status declined</b>, especially in upper-class city families. Upper-class girls had their feet tightly bound (<b>foot binding</b>), which crippled them for life; a woman with bound feet showed off her husband’s wealth because he could afford a “beautiful but impractical” wife. Peasant women, who worked in the fields, were affected less.</li></ul>

<div class="box"><h4>Answering the unit questions</h4><ul>
<li><b>Major achievements of the golden age:</b> reunification and expansion, the Grand Canal and roads, a revived civil service, movable type, gunpowder, porcelain, the compass, paper money, the mechanical clock, fast-ripening rice and terraces, huge ocean trade, Neo-Confucianism, and great poetry and painting.</li>
<li><b>How the exam led to a bureaucracy:</b> to run their empire the Tang revived the Han exam; those who passed became scholar-officials, an educated governing class chosen for talent instead of noble birth. They became the gentry, the new upper class.</li></ul></div>
<p class="meta">Tang vs. Song (Assessment #1). <b>Tang only:</b> large empire reaching Central Asia, Taizong and Wu Zhao, lost the Battle of Talas. <b>Song only:</b> smaller empire, lost the north to the Jurchen, capital at Hangzhou, most of the big inventions. <b>Both:</b> about 300 years each, strong central government, civil service exam, prosperity, and trade.</p>
