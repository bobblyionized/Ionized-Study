<h2>The Ming Dynasty</h2>
<p class="meta">Textbook pp. 617–623, stopping at “Manchus Found the Qing Dynasty” · Module 16, Lesson 3</p>
<div class="box"><h4>Big idea</h4>After driving out the Mongols, the <b>Ming Dynasty</b> (1368–1644) restored traditional Chinese values and made China the dominant power in Asia. It reached out to the world with Zheng He’s great voyages, then pulled back into isolation and kept outside influence to a minimum.</div>
<div class="tip"><b>Unit questions this lesson answers:</b> How did the Ming rise to power? Why were Zheng He’s voyages important? What allowed the Ming Dynasty to maintain stability?<br><b>Key terms:</b> Ming Dynasty, Hongwu, Yonglo, Zheng He, tribute</div>

<h3>China under the powerful Ming</h3>
<p>Under the Ming, China was the <b>dominant power in Asia</b>. Vassal states from Korea to Southeast Asia paid the Ming regular <b>tribute</b>: a payment from one country to another to show its submission. When Portuguese ships arrived off China’s coast in 1514, the Ming expected the Europeans to pay tribute too, and they would not let outsiders threaten the peace and prosperity they had brought after ending Mongol rule.</p>

<h3>The rise of the Ming: Hongwu</h3>
<ul><li><b>Hongwu</b>, the <b>son of a peasant</b>, commanded the rebel army that <b>drove the Mongols out of China in 1368</b>. That year he became the first Ming emperor, ruling from <b>Nanjing</b> in the south.</li>
<li>His reforms aimed to <b>restore farmland</b> ruined by war, <b>erase nearly every trace of the Mongol past</b>, and build China’s power and prosperity. He increased rice production, improved irrigation, and encouraged fish farming and cash crops like cotton and sugar cane.</li>
<li>He used respected <b>traditions</b> to bring stability: a <b>return to Confucian moral standards</b> and a restored, <b>merit-based civil service exam</b>.</li>
<li>Later in his rule, suspecting plots everywhere, he became a ruthless tyrant and purged the government, killing thousands of officials.</li></ul>

<h3>Yonglo and the Forbidden City</h3>
<p>Hongwu died in 1398. After a power struggle his son <b>Yonglo</b> took the throne. He kept many of his father’s policies but <b>moved the court to Beijing</b>.</p>
<p>There he built the <b>Forbidden City</b> (1406–1420), a huge palace complex surrounded by red walls 35 feet high, with dozens of palaces and temples. It got its name because <b>commoners and foreigners were not allowed in</b>, a way to show the emperor’s power and keep him apart from ordinary people. Its largest building, the <b>Hall of Supreme Harmony</b>, was used for great ceremonies, and its <b>Nine-Dragon Wall</b> shows dragons, the symbol of the imperial family.</p>

<h3>The voyages of Zheng He</h3>
<p>Yonglo was curious about the wider world. In <b>1405</b>, before European explorers sailed beyond their own waters, he launched the first of <b>seven voyages</b>. He wanted to <b>impress the world with Ming power and splendor</b> and to <b>expand China’s tribute system</b>.</p>
<ul><li>All seven were led by <b>Zheng He</b>, a <b>Chinese Muslim admiral</b>.</li>
<li>Everything was enormous: voyages from Southeast Asia to <b>eastern Africa</b>; 40 to 300 ships per trip; <b>treasure ships over 400 feet long</b> (Columbus’s Santa Maria was about 85 feet); crews of more than <b>27,000</b>, including sailors, soldiers, carpenters, interpreters, accountants, doctors, and religious leaders. The fleet was like a floating city.</li>
<li>Zheng He handed out gifts of silver and silk to show China’s superiority, and more than <b>16 countries</b> began sending tribute to the Ming.</li>
<li>Chinese scholar-officials complained that the voyages wasted money better spent defending the northern frontier. After the seventh voyage, in <b>1433</b>, <b>China withdrew into isolation</b>.</li></ul>

<h3>Ming relations with foreign countries</h3>
<ul><li>To keep foreign influence low, <b>only the government</b> could trade with foreigners, and only through three ports: <b>Canton, Macao, and Ningbo</b>.</li>
<li>In reality, merchants smuggled silk, porcelain, and other goods to eager Europeans, who usually paid in <b>silver</b>, much of it mined in the Americas.</li>
<li>Demand for Chinese goods grew industries like silk-making and ceramics, but China did <b>not industrialize</b>, for two reasons: commerce offended <b>Confucian beliefs</b>, and Chinese policy <b>favored agriculture</b> (low farm taxes, very high taxes on manufacturing and trade).</li>
<li>Christian missionaries came with European traders, bringing Christianity and European science and technology such as the clock. The Italian Jesuit <b>Matteo Ricci</b> won favor at court with his intelligence and fluent Chinese, but many educated Chinese opposed the European presence.</li>
<li>Fearing a Mongol invasion, the Ming rebuilt and extended the <b>Great Wall</b> more than any dynasty before. Most of the wall we see today is Ming.</li></ul>

<h3>A stable and diverse society</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>What kept the Ming stable</h4><ul>
<li><b>A Confucian government:</b> Hongwu replaced the Yuan laws with new codes based on Confucian teachings and made the emperor head of the state. With a strong leader this was very stable (it worked less well under weak ones).</li>
<li><b>Confucian exams:</b> candidates for high office had to pass an exam on Confucian texts, so officials were highly literate and competent.</li>
<li><b>The Hanlin Academy</b>, a Tang-era institution, issued the official reading of the Confucian books. Hongwu turned it into <b>six secretaries</b> who governed parts of the empire and reported directly to him, giving him direct control.</li>
<li><b>The Censorate</b> (by 1430) enforced anti-corruption laws. Its officials inspected the provinces and could remove corrupt officials, which pushed officials to be honest.</li>
<li><b>The military</b> managed diverse provinces, for example by moving more Han Chinese into Yunnan.</li>
<li><b>Religious diversity</b>: Buddhism, Confucianism, and Daoism all flourished with new temples. Daoist priests performed court rituals and wrote hymns for the emperor, Buddhist monks led rituals for common people, Jesuits served as court musicians, astronomers, and mapmakers, and many sects blended in Confucian ideals.</li></ul></div>

<div class="box"><h4>Answering the unit questions</h4><ul>
<li><b>How did the Ming rise to power?</b> Hongwu, a peasant’s son, led the rebel army that drove the Mongols out in 1368, became the first Ming emperor, rebuilt farming, erased Mongol influence, and restored Confucian values and the civil service exam.</li>
<li><b>Why were Zheng He’s voyages important?</b> They showed off Ming power from Southeast Asia to East Africa decades before European explorers, brought more than 16 countries into the tribute system, and, when they ended in 1433, marked China’s turn toward isolation.</li>
<li><b>What kept the Ming stable?</b> A strong emperor with Confucian law codes, competent officials chosen by Confucian exams, the Hanlin Academy’s six secretaries reporting to the emperor, the Censorate fighting corruption, the military in diverse provinces, and religious tolerance.</li></ul></div>
<p class="meta">What comes next (the reading stops here): by 1600 the Ming had weakened, and in 1644 the Manchus from the northeast invaded and founded the Qing Dynasty.</p>
