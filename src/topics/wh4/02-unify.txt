<h2>The Unification of China</h2>
<p class="meta">Textbook pp. 137–141 · Module 4, Lesson 4</p>
<div class="box"><h4>Big idea</h4>The disorder of the warring states led Chinese thinkers to create three ethical systems: <b>Confucianism</b>, <b>Daoism</b>, and <b>Legalism</b>. Their ideas still shape China today.</div>
<div class="tip"><b>Unit questions this lesson answers:</b> Why and how did Confucianism develop? What are the basic beliefs of Confucians? How do Legalism and Daoism compare to Confucianism?<br><b>Key terms:</b> Confucius, filial piety, Analects, bureaucracy, Daoism, Legalism, I Ching, yin and yang</div>

<h3>Setting the stage: the warring states</h3>
<p>The <b>Zhou Dynasty</b> ruled for about eight centuries (c. 1027–256 BC). For its first 300 years the Zhou kings controlled a large empire, and local rulers answered to the king. Later, the lords of these territories began acting like independent kings and fought each other almost constantly. This <b>warring states period</b> brought the Zhou down.</p>
<p>In the chaos, China drifted away from its old values of <b>social order, harmony, and respect for authority</b>. Scholars and philosophers proposed different ways to bring those values back.</p>

<h3>Confucius and the social order</h3>
<p><b>Confucius</b> (551–479 BC) was China’s most influential scholar. Born into a poor family while the Zhou was in decline, he earned his living as a teacher of history, music, and moral character. He wanted to restore the order and moral living of earlier times.</p>
<div class="box"><h4>The five relationships</h4>Confucius believed social order, harmony, and good government would return if society were organized around five relationships, each with its own code of proper conduct:
<ol><li>ruler and subject</li><li>father and son</li><li>husband and wife</li><li>older brother and younger brother</li><li>friend and friend</li></ol>
For example, rulers should be kind and live virtuously; in return, subjects should be loyal and obey the law. <b>Three of the five are family relationships.</b></div>
<p><b>Filial piety</b> is respect for parents and ancestors. It meant devoting yourself to your parents while they lived and honoring their memory with rituals after they died. In the <i>Analects</i>, Confucius points out that people also feed their dogs and horses; what makes caring for parents different is <b>respect</b>.</p>
<p>The duke of Lu made Confucius minister of justice, and legend says crime nearly vanished because of his good example. When the duke’s ways changed, Confucius resigned. He spent the rest of his life teaching and thought of himself as a failure because he never held high office. His students collected his sayings in a book called the <b>Analects</b>, and a later follower, <b>Mencius</b>, spread his ideas.</p>

<h3>Confucian ideas about government</h3>
<ul><li>Confucius taught that <b>education</b> could turn even a humbly born person into a gentleman. This laid the groundwork for a <b>bureaucracy</b>: a trained civil service, the people who run the government.</li>
<li>A gentleman had four virtues: courteous in private life, precise in serving his master, generous in providing for the people, and fair when asking the people to serve.</li>
<li>Education became the key to advancing in government.</li>
<li>Confucianism is an <b>ethical system</b> (a system of right and wrong); scholars still debate whether it is a religion. It became the foundation of Chinese government and social order and spread across East Asia.</li></ul>

<h3>Daoism: harmony with nature</h3>
<p><b>Laozi</b>, who may have lived in the 500s BC (no one is sure he existed), taught that only the <b>natural order</b> matters. His book, the <i>Dao De Jing</i> (The Way of Virtue), says a universal force called the <b>Dao</b>, “the Way,” guides all things. His philosophy is called <b>Daoism</b>.</p>
<ul><li>Of all creatures, only humans fail to follow the Dao: they argue about right and wrong, which Laozi saw as pointless.</li>
<li>Government should do <b>as little as possible</b> and leave people alone. People should live simply and in harmony with nature.</li>
<li>Daoists studied nature closely, which led to contributions in alchemy, astronomy, and medicine.</li>
<li>Laozi’s own writing says that if powerful people centered themselves in the Dao, the world would transform naturally and people would be content and free of desire. So Daoists would <b>disapprove of a leader who builds an empire</b>.</li></ul>

<h3>Legalism: strict laws and harsh punishments</h3>
<p>The <b>Legalists</b> were practical political thinkers. They believed a <b>highly efficient and powerful government</b> was the key to restoring order, using the <b>law</b> to end disorder. <b>Hanfeizi</b> and <b>Li Si</b> were among its founders.</p>
<ul><li>Reward people who do their duties well; punish the disobedient harshly. In practice they stressed <b>punishment</b> (for example, losing your ears or nose for leaving your village without a travel permit).</li>
<li>Control ideas as well as actions: burn writings that might lead people to criticize the government. The ruler governs; the people obey.</li>
<li>A prince of the new Qin Dynasty adopted Legalist ideas and used them to bring order to China.</li></ul>

<h3>The I Ching and yin and yang</h3>
<p>People less interested in these debates found answers elsewhere. The <b>I Ching</b> (Book of Changes) was a book of oracles: readers threw coins, interpreted the result, and read the matching prediction for good advice and common sense. The idea of <b>yin and yang</b> described two forces that together make up the rhythm of life. <b>Yin</b> is cold, dark, soft, and mysterious; <b>yang</b> is warm, bright, hard, and clear. The circle symbol shows them in balance, completing each other. Both helped people understand how they fit into the world.</p>

<h3>Comparing the three ethical systems</h3>
<table class="exmix"><tr><th></th><th>Confucianism</th><th>Daoism</th><th>Legalism</th></tr>
<tr><td>When</td><td>Developed 551–479 BC</td><td>Started around 600–500 BC</td><td>Developed in the 400s BC</td></tr>
<tr><td>Founders</td><td>Confucius; spread by Mencius</td><td>Laozi</td><td>Hanfeizi, Li Si</td></tr>
<tr><td>Key to order</td><td>Family relationships and proper conduct</td><td>The natural order matters more than the social order</td><td>A powerful, efficient government</td></tr>
<tr><td>Beliefs</td><td>Respect parents and elders; education matters for the person and society</td><td>A universal force (the Dao) guides all things; live simply, in harmony with nature</td><td>Punishments keep order; thinkers and ideas must be strictly controlled</td></tr>
<tr><td>Government</td><td>Rule by good example; educated officials</td><td>Govern as little as possible</td><td>Strict laws, rewards, and harsh punishment</td></tr></table>
<p><b>Which stress government and a well-ordered society?</b> Confucianism and Legalism. <b>Which is most moderate and balanced?</b> Confucianism: it values order like Legalism but builds it on respect, family, and education instead of fear.</p>

<div class="box"><h4>Answering the unit questions</h4><ul>
<li><b>Why and how did Confucianism develop?</b> The warring states period destroyed order and respect for authority. Confucius, teaching as the Zhou declined, wanted to restore the moral order of earlier times; his students recorded his teachings in the Analects and Mencius spread them.</li>
<li><b>Basic beliefs of Confucians:</b> the five relationships with proper conduct in each; filial piety; rulers lead by virtue and good example; education makes a gentleman and a good official.</li>
<li><b>How do Legalism and Daoism compare?</b> Legalism agrees that order matters but enforces it with strict laws and harsh punishment instead of virtue. Daoism rejects the focus on social order: follow nature and the Dao, and let government do as little as possible.</li></ul></div>
