<h2>The Qin Dynasty Unifies China</h2>
<p class="meta">Textbook pp. 141–143 · Module 4, Lesson 4 (continued)</p>
<div class="box"><h4>Big idea</h4>Using Legalist ideas, Shi Huangdi ended the warring states and unified China (221–202 BC) with a harsh, all-powerful government. He built roads, standardized the country, and joined the Great Wall, but his cruelty made the Qin hated, and it collapsed soon after he died.</div>
<div class="tip"><b>Unit questions this lesson answers:</b> How did Shi Huangdi rule China? How did the Qin Dynasty fall? Why was the Great Wall of China significant?<br><b>Key terms:</b> Qin Dynasty, Shi Huangdi, autocracy, Great Wall of China</div>

<h3>A new emperor takes control</h3>
<p>In the third century BC the <b>Qin Dynasty</b> (said “chihn”), from the western state of Qin, replaced the Zhou. Its founder used <b>Legalist</b> ideas to defeat the warring states. In <b>221 BC</b>, after ruling for over 20 years, he took the name <b>Shi Huangdi</b>, meaning <b>“First Emperor.”</b></p>
<ul><li>He first stopped the internal battles that had drained China’s strength.</li>
<li>His armies fought invaders north of the Huang He (Yellow River) and pushed south as far as present-day Vietnam, <b>doubling China’s size</b>.</li></ul>

<h3>Crushing opposition: an autocracy</h3>
<ul><li><b>“Strengthening the trunk and weakening the branches”:</b> to break the power of rival warlords, he forced noble families to live in the capital where he could watch them. Tradition says 120,000 families were uprooted.</li>
<li>He took their land and divided China into <b>36 administrative districts</b> run by Qin officials.</li>
<li>To stop criticism, he and his Legalist prime minister <b>Li Si</b> killed hundreds of <b>Confucian scholars</b> and <b>burned “useless” books</b> (Confucian works and poetry). Practical books on medicine and farming were spared.</li></ul>
<p>The result was an <b>autocracy</b>: a government with unlimited power that uses it in an arbitrary way.</p>

<h3>A program of centralization</h3>
<ul><li>A highway network of more than <b>4,000 miles</b>.</li>
<li>One standard across China for <b>writing, law, currency, and weights and measures</b>, even the length of cart axles, so every cart fit the ruts of the main roads.</li>
<li>Irrigation projects increased farm production; trade grew thanks to the roads, and a new class of <b>merchants</b> became important.</li></ul>
<p>Despite these advances, <b>harsh taxes and a repressive government</b> made the Qin unpopular. Shi Huangdi unified China at the expense of human freedom. Still, he is considered the <b>founder of unified China</b>, and the word <b>“China” comes from “Qin.”</b></p>

<h3>The Great Wall of China</h3>
<p>Scholars hated Shi Huangdi for burning books; poor people hated him for forcing them to build a huge wall. Earlier Zhou rulers had built smaller walls against <b>northern nomads</b>. Shi Huangdi closed the gaps and extended the wall almost the length of the empire’s border: an enemy would have to ride halfway to Tibet to get around it.</p>
<ul><li>Built by <b>hundreds of thousands of peasants</b> who were not paid and did not work out of love for the empire: the choice was work on the wall or die. Many died anyway from the crushing labor and harsh winters.</li>
<li>It stretched from the Yellow Sea in the east to the Gobi Desert in the west, with watchtowers every 200–300 yards.</li>
<li>Sections were 20–25 feet high: cut stone slabs outside, packed with dirt, pebbles, and rubble inside.</li>
<li>Most of the wall standing today was rebuilt later, by the <b>Ming Dynasty</b> (AD 1368–1644).</li></ul>
<p>Shi Huangdi also built a burial chamber the size of a large town, guarded by <b>8,000 life-sized clay soldiers</b>, the <b>terra-cotta warriors</b>.</p>

<h3>The fall of the Qin</h3>
<p>The Qin lasted only a short time (<b>221–202 BC</b>). Shi Huangdi died in 210 BC. His son was just as cruel but less able. Peasants rebelled only three years after the second emperor took power. A rebel leader, a peasant from the land of Han, marched on the capital, and by <b>202 BC</b> the Qin gave way to the <b>Han Dynasty</b>, one of the longest in Chinese history.</p>

<div class="box"><h4>Answering the unit questions</h4><ul>
<li><b>How did Shi Huangdi rule?</b> As an autocrat using Legalism: he crushed nobles and critics (moving nobles to the capital, killing scholars, burning books), split China into 36 districts under his officials, and centralized everything with roads and standard writing, laws, money, and measures.</li>
<li><b>How did the Qin fall?</b> Harsh taxes, forced labor, and repression made it hated. After Shi Huangdi died, his weaker son faced peasant rebellions within three years, and by 202 BC the Han took over.</li>
<li><b>Why was the Great Wall significant?</b> It defended China’s northern border against nomads and showed the emperor’s power to command huge numbers of people, but it was built with forced labor that cost many lives and turned the poor against the Qin.</li></ul></div>
<div class="warn"><b>Positive and negative effects of Shi Huangdi’s rule (Reading Check).</b> Positive: a unified, larger China; roads; standard writing, laws, money, and measures; more farming and trade; the Great Wall. Negative: no freedom, murdered scholars, burned books, crushing taxes, forced labor, and a dynasty that lasted only about 20 years.</div>
