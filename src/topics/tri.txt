<script type="text/plain" id="L-tri-angle">
<h2>Angle Chasing</h2>
<div class="box"><h4>Facts</h4><ul>
<li>Angles of a triangle sum to $180^\circ$; an $n$-gon's interior angles sum to $180(n-2)^\circ$.</li>
<li><b>Exterior angle</b> $=$ sum of the two remote interior angles.</li>
<li>Isosceles: equal sides face equal angles.</li>
<li>Parallel lines with a transversal: alternate interior and corresponding angles are equal, and same-side interior angles are supplementary.</li>
<li>Angle bisectors of $B$ and $C$ meet at the incenter $I$, with $\angle BIC=90^\circ+\frac A2$.</li>
</ul></div>
<h3>Technique</h3>
<p>Label one unknown angle $x$ and express everything else in terms of it. Isosceles triangles, especially ones created by equal segments like $BD=BC$, give the equations. Write every angle you know on the diagram.</p>
<div class="ex"><b class="lbl">Example.</b> What is the sum of the five tip angles of a five-pointed star?
<details><summary>Solution</summary>Each tip angle plus two exterior-angle relations lets you collapse the five angles into one triangle, so the sum is $180^\circ$.</details></div>
</script>
<script type="text/plain" id="L-tri-sim">
<h2>Similar Triangles</h2>
<div class="box"><h4>Key facts</h4><ul>
<li><b>AA:</b> two equal angles make triangles similar. Parallel lines and shared angles create them.</li>
<li>With scale factor $k$: lengths scale by $k$, areas by $k^2$, volumes by $k^3$.</li>
<li>If $DE\parallel BC$ with $D$ on $AB$ and $E$ on $AC$: $\frac{AD}{AB}=\frac{AE}{AC}=\frac{DE}{BC}$.</li>
<li>Right triangle with altitude $h$ to the hypotenuse, which splits it into $p$ and $q$: $h^2=pq$, $a^2=pc$, $b^2=qc$, and $h=\frac{ab}{c}$.</li>
</ul></div>
<h3>Classics</h3>
<ul><li>Shadows: $\frac{\text{height}}{\text{shadow}}$ is the same for all objects at the same moment.</li>
<li>A line parallel to the base that halves the area cuts the sides at ratio $\frac1{\sqrt2}$ from the apex.</li>
<li>A square in the right angle of a right triangle with legs $a,b$ has side $\frac{ab}{a+b}$.</li>
<li>Trapezoid diagonals cut each other in the ratio of the bases.</li></ul>
</script>
<script type="text/plain" id="L-tri-pyth">
<h2>Pythagorean Theorem &amp; Special Triangles</h2>
<div class="box"><h4>Know these</h4><ul>
<li>Triples: $3\text{-}4\text{-}5$, $5\text{-}12\text{-}13$, $8\text{-}15\text{-}17$, $7\text{-}24\text{-}25$, $20\text{-}21\text{-}29$, $9\text{-}40\text{-}41$, and their multiples.</li>
<li>$45$-$45$-$90$: sides $1:1:\sqrt2$. $\quad30$-$60$-$90$: sides $1:\sqrt3:2$.</li>
<li>Equilateral triangle of side $s$: height $\frac{\sqrt3}{2}s$, area $\frac{\sqrt3}{4}s^2$.</li>
<li>Square of side $s$: diagonal $s\sqrt2$. Box: space diagonal $\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}$.</li>
</ul></div>
<h3>Acute, right, or obtuse?</h3>
<p>With longest side $c$: $a^2+b^2>c^2$ means acute, $=$ means right, $\lt$ means obtuse.</p>
<h3>Counting integer right triangles with a given leg</h3>
<p>$c^2-b^2=a^2$ means $(c-b)(c+b)=a^2$. Count factor pairs of $a^2$ of equal parity with $c-b\lt c+b$.</p>
</script>
<script type="text/plain" id="L-tri-area">
<h2>Triangle Area Formulas</h2>
<table class="t"><tr><th>Formula</th><th>Use when</th></tr>
<tr><td>$\frac12bh$</td><td>a base and its height are known</td></tr>
<tr><td>$\frac12ab\sin C$</td><td>two sides and the included angle</td></tr>
<tr><td>Heron: $\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}$, $s=\frac{a+b+c}2$</td><td>all three sides</td></tr>
<tr><td>$rs$</td><td>inradius and semiperimeter</td></tr>
<tr><td>$\frac{abc}{4R}$</td><td>circumradius</td></tr>
<tr><td>Shoelace</td><td>vertex coordinates</td></tr></table>
<h3>Memorize</h3>
<p>The $13$-$14$-$15$ triangle has area $84$ (it is a $5$-$12$-$13$ and a $9$-$12$-$15$ glued along the height $12$). Other integer-area triangles: $5$-$5$-$6$ (12), $5$-$5$-$8$ (12), $10$-$13$-$13$ (60).</p>
<h3>Viviani's theorem</h3>
<p>From any interior point of an equilateral triangle, the distances to the three sides add up to the height.</p>
<h3>Maximum area</h3>
<p>Two sides $a,b$ give area at most $\frac12ab$, reached when they are perpendicular.</p>
</script>
<script type="text/plain" id="L-tri-cev">
<h2>Angle Bisectors, Medians &amp; Cevians</h2>
<div class="box"><h4>Theorems</h4><ul>
<li><b>Angle bisector theorem:</b> the bisector from $A$ hits $BC$ at $D$ with $\frac{BD}{DC}=\frac{AB}{AC}$.</li>
<li><b>Bisector length:</b> $AD^2=bc\left[1-\left(\frac{a}{b+c}\right)^2\right]$.</li>
<li><b>Median length:</b> $m_a=\frac12\sqrt{2b^2+2c^2-a^2}$.</li>
<li><b>Stewart's theorem:</b> for a cevian $AD=d$ with $BD=m$ and $DC=n$ ($m+n=a$): $b^2m+c^2n=a(d^2+mn)$.</li>
<li><b>Centroid</b> $G$: divides each median $2:1$ from the vertex, and is the average of the vertex coordinates.</li>
<li>The three medians cut a triangle into six triangles of equal area.</li>
<li>In a right triangle, the median to the hypotenuse is half the hypotenuse.</li>
</ul></div>
<p>Mnemonic for Stewart: "a man and his dad put a bomb in the sink" gives $man+dad=bmb+cnc$.</p>
</script>
<script type="text/plain" id="L-tri-center">
<h2>Inradius, Circumradius &amp; Centers</h2>
<div class="box"><h4>Formulas</h4><ul>
<li>Inradius $r=\dfrac{\text{Area}}{s}$; circumradius $R=\dfrac{abc}{4\cdot\text{Area}}=\dfrac{a}{2\sin A}$.</li>
<li>Right triangle (legs $a,b$, hypotenuse $c$): $R=\frac c2$ and $r=\frac{a+b-c}{2}$.</li>
<li>Equilateral (side $s$): $R=\frac{s}{\sqrt3}$ and $r=\frac{s}{2\sqrt3}$, so $R=2r$.</li>
<li>Tangent lengths: the incircle touches the side opposite $A$, splitting it into $s-b$ and $s-c$. The tangent length from $A$ is $s-a$.</li>
</ul></div>
<h3>The four classic centers</h3>
<ul><li><b>Centroid</b> (medians), <b>incenter</b> (angle bisectors), <b>circumcenter</b> (perpendicular bisectors), <b>orthocenter</b> (altitudes).</li>
<li>In a right triangle the circumcenter is the midpoint of the hypotenuse and the orthocenter is the right-angle vertex.</li></ul>
</script>

Q: In triangle $ABC$, $AB=AC$ and $\angle A=36^\circ$. Point $D$ on $AC$ satisfies $BD=BC$. What is $\angle ABD$, in degrees?

(So $ABD$ is isosceles too: $AD=BD$. This is the "golden triangle.")
