<script type="text/plain" id="L-polyg-quad">
<h2>Quadrilaterals</h2>
<table class="t"><tr><th>Shape</th><th>Key facts</th><th>Area</th></tr>
<tr><td>Parallelogram</td><td>opposite sides equal and parallel; diagonals bisect each other</td><td>$bh=ab\sin\theta$</td></tr>
<tr><td>Rectangle</td><td>diagonals equal: $d^2=a^2+b^2$</td><td>$ab$</td></tr>
<tr><td>Rhombus</td><td>diagonals perpendicular bisectors: side$^2=\left(\frac{d_1}2\right)^2+\left(\frac{d_2}2\right)^2$</td><td>$\frac{d_1d_2}{2}$</td></tr>
<tr><td>Kite</td><td>one diagonal is the perpendicular bisector of the other</td><td>$\frac{d_1d_2}{2}$</td></tr>
<tr><td>Trapezoid</td><td>midsegment $=\frac{a+b}2$</td><td>$\frac{a+b}{2}h$</td></tr></table>
<h3>Tricks</h3>
<ul><li><b>Isosceles trapezoid:</b> drop heights from the short base. Each overhang is $\frac{b-a}2$, and Pythagoras with the leg gives $h$.</li>
<li><b>Rectangle from perimeter and diagonal:</b> $a+b=\frac P2$ and $a^2+b^2=d^2$, so $ab=\frac{(a+b)^2-d^2}{2}$.</li>
<li><b>Varignon:</b> the midpoints of any quadrilateral form a parallelogram with half the area.</li>
<li>Any triangle with a base on one side of a parallelogram and its apex on the opposite side has half the parallelogram's area.</li></ul>
</script>
<script type="text/plain" id="L-polyg-reg">
<h2>Regular Polygons</h2>
<div class="box"><h4>For a regular $n$-gon</h4><ul>
<li>Interior angle sum $180(n-2)$; each interior angle $\frac{180(n-2)}{n}$; each exterior angle $\frac{360}{n}$.</li>
<li>Number of diagonals $\frac{n(n-3)}{2}$.</li>
<li>Hexagon of side $s$: six equilateral triangles, area $\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}s^2$. The long diagonal is $2s$ and the short diagonal is $s\sqrt3$.</li>
<li>Octagon of side $s$: area $2(1+\sqrt2)s^2$ (a square with its corners cut off).</li>
<li>Area $=\frac12\cdot$perimeter$\cdot$apothem.</li>
</ul></div>
<p>To find $n$ from an angle, use the exterior angle: $n=\frac{360}{\text{exterior}}$. An interior angle of $150^\circ$ means an exterior of $30^\circ$, so $n=12$.</p>
</script>
<script type="text/plain" id="L-polyg-shoe">
<h2>Shoelace Formula &amp; Pick's Theorem</h2>
<div class="box"><h4>Shoelace</h4>
For vertices $(x_1,y_1),\dots,(x_n,y_n)$ in order around the polygon:
$$\text{Area}=\frac12\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_iy_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i)\right|$$ (indices wrap around).</div>
<div class="box"><h4>Pick's theorem</h4>
A lattice polygon with $I$ interior and $B$ boundary lattice points has area $A=I+\frac B2-1$.</div>
<h3>Lattice points on a segment</h3>
<p>The segment from $(x_1,y_1)$ to $(x_2,y_2)$ contains $\gcd(|\Delta x|,|\Delta y|)+1$ lattice points, including both endpoints.</p>
<h3>Typical use</h3>
<p>To count interior points: find the area by shoelace, count $B$ with gcds, then $I=A-\frac B2+1$.</p>
</script>
<script type="text/plain" id="L-polyg-ratio">
<h2>Area Ratios &amp; Mass Points</h2>
<div class="box"><h4>Same height, so area ratio equals base ratio</h4>
If $D$ is on $BC$, then $\frac{[ABD]}{[ADC]}=\frac{BD}{DC}$. Chain these: each cevian splits areas in the ratio of the segments it creates.</div>
<h3>Mass points</h3>
<ol><li>On segment $BC$ with $BD:DC=p:q$, put mass $q$ at $B$ and $p$ at $C$ (masses are inversely proportional to the lengths).</li>
<li>Mass at $D$ $=$ the sum, $p+q$.</li>
<li>Where two cevians cross, the ratio along a cevian from vertex $A$ is $AP:PD=m_D:m_A$.</li>
<li>Scale the masses so the shared vertex has the same mass in both cevians.</li></ol>
<h3>Other ratio facts</h3>
<ul><li>A trapezoid's diagonals create triangles at the two bases whose areas are in ratio $\left(\frac ab\right)^2$.</li>
<li>Points at fraction $t$ along each side (in the same direction) form a triangle with $1-3t(1-t)$ of the area.</li>
<li>Midpoint constructions often cut areas in half repeatedly.</li></ul>
</script>
