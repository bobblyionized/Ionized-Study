<script type="text/plain" id="L-polyg-quad">
<h2>Quadrilaterals</h2>
<table class="t"><tr><th>Shape</th><th>Key facts</th><th>Area</th></tr>
<tr><td>Parallelogram</td><td>opposite sides equal and parallel; diagonals bisect each other</td><td>$bh=ab\sin\theta$</td></tr>
<tr><td>Rectangle</td><td>diagonals equal: $d^2=a^2+b^2$</td><td>$ab$</td></tr>
<tr><td>Rhombus</td><td>diagonals perpendicular bisectors: side$^2=\left(\frac{d_1}2\right)^2+\left(\frac{d_2}2\right)^2$</td><td>$\frac{d_1d_2}{2}$</td></tr>
<tr><td>Kite</td><td>one diagonal is the perpendicular bisector of the other</td><td>$\frac{d_1d_2}{2}$</td></tr>
<tr><td>Trapezoid</td><td>midsegment $=\frac{a+b}2$</td><td>$\frac{a+b}{2}h$</td></tr></table>
<h3>Tricks</h3>
<ul><li><b>Isosceles trapezoid:</b> drop heights from the short base. Each overhang is $\frac{b-a}2$, and Pythagoras with the leg gives $h$.</li>
<li><b>Rectangle from perimeter and diagonal:</b> $a+b=\frac P2$ and $a^2+b^2=d^2$, so $ab=\frac{(a+b)^2-d^2}{2}$.</li>
<li><b>Varignon:</b> the midpoints of any quadrilateral form a parallelogram with half the area.</li>
<li>Any triangle with a base on one side of a parallelogram and its apex on the opposite side has half the parallelogram's area.</li></ul>
</script>
<script type="text/plain" id="L-polyg-reg">
<h2>Regular Polygons</h2>
<div class="box"><h4>For a regular $n$-gon</h4><ul>
<li>Interior angle sum $180(n-2)$; each interior angle $\frac{180(n-2)}{n}$; each exterior angle $\frac{360}{n}$.</li>
<li>Number of diagonals $\frac{n(n-3)}{2}$.</li>
<li>Hexagon of side $s$: six equilateral triangles, area $\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}s^2$. The long diagonal is $2s$ and the short diagonal is $s\sqrt3$.</li>
<li>Octagon of side $s$: area $2(1+\sqrt2)s^2$ (a square with its corners cut off).</li>
<li>Area $=\frac12\cdot$perimeter$\cdot$apothem.</li>
</ul></div>
<p>To find $n$ from an angle, use the exterior angle: $n=\frac{360}{\text{exterior}}$. An interior angle of $150^\circ$ means an exterior of $30^\circ$, so $n=12$.</p>
</script>
<script type="text/plain" id="L-polyg-shoe">
<h2>Shoelace Formula &amp; Pick's Theorem</h2>
<div class="box"><h4>Shoelace</h4>
For vertices $(x_1,y_1),\dots,(x_n,y_n)$ in order around the polygon:
$$\text{Area}=\frac12\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_iy_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i)\right|$$ (indices wrap around).</div>
<div class="box"><h4>Pick's theorem</h4>
A lattice polygon with $I$ interior and $B$ boundary lattice points has area $A=I+\frac B2-1$.</div>
<h3>Lattice points on a segment</h3>
<p>The segment from $(x_1,y_1)$ to $(x_2,y_2)$ contains $\gcd(|\Delta x|,|\Delta y|)+1$ lattice points, including both endpoints.</p>
<h3>Typical use</h3>
<p>To count interior points: find the area by shoelace, count $B$ with gcds, then $I=A-\frac B2+1$.</p>
</script>
<script type="text/plain" id="L-polyg-ratio">
<h2>Area Ratios &amp; Mass Points</h2>
<div class="box"><h4>Same height, so area ratio equals base ratio</h4>
If $D$ is on $BC$, then $\frac{[ABD]}{[ADC]}=\frac{BD}{DC}$. Chain these: each cevian splits areas in the ratio of the segments it creates.</div>
<h3>Mass points</h3>
<ol><li>On segment $BC$ with $BD:DC=p:q$, put mass $q$ at $B$ and $p$ at $C$ (masses are inversely proportional to the lengths).</li>
<li>Mass at $D$ $=$ the sum, $p+q$.</li>
<li>Where two cevians cross, the ratio along a cevian from vertex $A$ is $AP:PD=m_D:m_A$.</li>
<li>Scale the masses so the shared vertex has the same mass in both cevians.</li></ol>
<h3>Other ratio facts</h3>
<ul><li>A trapezoid's diagonals create triangles at the two bases whose areas are in ratio $\left(\frac ab\right)^2$.</li>
<li>Points at fraction $t$ along each side (in the same direction) form a triangle with $1-3t(1-t)$ of the area.</li>
<li>Midpoint constructions often cut areas in half repeatedly.</li></ul>
</script>

Q: A rhombus has diagonals $10$ and $24$. What is its side length?
L: 1
A: 13
W: 17
W: 26
W: 120
W: 14
S: The diagonals of a rhombus bisect each other at right angles, splitting it into four congruent right triangles.
Each triangle has legs equal to half the diagonals, $5$ and $12$, and its hypotenuse is a side of the rhombus.
Side $=\sqrt{5^2+12^2}=\sqrt{169}=13$.
---
Q: A rhombus has diagonals $10$ and $24$. What is its area?
L: 1
A: 120
W: 240
W: 60
W: 34
W: 169
S: A rhombus (like any quadrilateral with perpendicular diagonals) has area $\frac{d_1d_2}{2}$.
Reason: the four right triangles have total area $4\cdot\frac12\cdot\frac{d_1}2\cdot\frac{d_2}2=\frac{d_1d_2}{2}$.
Area $=\frac{10\cdot24}{2}=120$.

W: 110
W: 70
W: 40
W: 60
S: Trapezoid area $=$ (average of the bases) $\times$ height.
$\frac{8+14}{2}\cdot5=11\cdot5=55$.

Q: An isosceles trapezoid has bases $10$ and $22$ and legs of length $10$. What is its area?
L: 2
A: 128
W: 160
W: 96
W: 136
W: 112
S: Drop perpendiculars from the ends of the short base. They cut off two congruent right triangles, each with horizontal leg ("overhang") $\frac{22-10}{2}=6$ and hypotenuse $10$ (the leg).
Height: $h=\sqrt{10^2-6^2}=\sqrt{64}=8$.
Area $=\frac{10+22}{2}\cdot8=16\cdot8=128$.

Q: What is the area of a regular octagon with side length $1$?
L: 3
A: 2+2\sqrt{2}
W: 4
W: 2+\sqrt{2}
W: 1+2\sqrt{2}
W: 3\sqrt{2}
S: Extend four alternate sides to form a square around the octagon. Each corner of the square cuts off a right isosceles triangle whose hypotenuse is a side of the octagon, $1$, so its legs are $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$.
The square's side is $\frac{\sqrt2}2+1+\frac{\sqrt2}2=1+\sqrt2$, with area $(1+\sqrt2)^2=3+2\sqrt2$.
The four corner triangles have area $4\cdot\frac12\cdot\left(\frac{\sqrt2}2\right)^2=1$.
Octagon area: $3+2\sqrt2-1=2+2\sqrt2$.

Q: What is the area of the quadrilateral with vertices $(0,0)$, $(4,0)$, $(5,3)$, $(1,4)$ (in that order)?
L: 2
A: \frac{29}{2}
W: 29
W: 14
W: 15
W: \frac{27}{2}
S: Shoelace formula: Area $=\frac12\left|\sum(x_iy_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i)\right|$, going around the vertices in order and back to the start.
• $(0,0)\to(4,0)$: $0\cdot0-4\cdot0=0$
• $(4,0)\to(5,3)$: $4\cdot3-5\cdot0=12$
• $(5,3)\to(1,4)$: $5\cdot4-1\cdot3=17$
• $(1,4)\to(0,0)$: $1\cdot0-0\cdot4=0$
Sum $29$, so the area is $\frac{29}{2}$.

Q: Point $D$ is on side $BC$ of triangle $ABC$ with $BD:DC=2:3$. If the area of $ABC$ is $50$, what is the area of $ABD$?
L: 1
A: 20
W: 30
W: 25
W: 40
W: 10
S: Triangles $ABD$ and $ABC$ share the vertex $A$, so they have the same height to line $BC$.
Their areas are proportional to their bases: $\frac{BD}{BC}=\frac{2}{2+3}=\frac25$.
$[ABD]=\frac25\cdot50=20$.
---
Q: In triangle $ABC$, $D$ is the midpoint of $BC$ and $E$ is the midpoint of $AD$. What fraction of the area of $ABC$ is triangle $ABE$?
L: 1
A: \frac{1}{4}
W: \frac{1}{2}
W: \frac{1}{3}
W: \frac{1}{8}
W: \frac{1}{6}
S: Median $AD$ splits $ABC$ into two equal halves: $[ABD]=\frac12[ABC]$ (same height, equal bases $BD=DC$).
In triangle $ABD$, $BE$ is a median (since $E$ is the midpoint of $AD$), so $[ABE]=\frac12[ABD]$.
$[ABE]=\frac12\cdot\frac12[ABC]=\frac14[ABC]$.
---
Q: In triangle $ABC$, $D$ is on $BC$ with $BD:DC=1:2$, and $E$ is the midpoint of $AC$. Cevians $AD$ and $BE$ meet at $P$. What is $\dfrac{AP}{PD}$?
L: 2
A: 3
W: 2
W: \frac{3}{2}
W: 4
W: \frac{3}{4}
S: Mass points: put masses so each cevian's foot is a balance point.
On $BC$: $BD:DC=1:2$ means $m_B\cdot1=m_C\cdot2$, so take $m_B=2$, $m_C=1$; then $m_D=m_B+m_C=3$.
On $AC$: $E$ is the midpoint, so $m_A=m_C=1$.
On $AD$, the balance point $P$ divides it inversely to the masses: $\frac{AP}{PD}=\frac{m_D}{m_A}=\frac31=3$.
---
Q: Parallelogram $ABCD$ has area $60$, and $P$ is any point on side $CD$. What is the area of triangle $ABP$?
L: 1
A: 30
W: 20
W: 15
W: 60
W: 40
S: Triangle $ABP$ has base $AB$, and its height is the distance from $P$ to line $AB$, which is the parallelogram's height (since $P$ is on the opposite side $CD$).
So $[ABP]=\frac12\cdot AB\cdot h=\frac12[ABCD]=30$, wherever $P$ is on $CD$.
---
Q: A triangle has area $36$. Points are chosen one-third of the way along each side (all in the same rotational direction), forming an inner triangle. What is its area?
L: 3
A: 12
W: 9
W: 18
W: 4
W: 16
S: Each corner triangle shares an angle with the big triangle, with the two sides around it being $\frac13$ and $\frac23$ of the original sides.
Since area scales with the product of those two sides ($\frac12ab\sin C$), each corner triangle has $\frac13\cdot\frac23=\frac29$ of the whole area.
Inner triangle: $1-3\cdot\frac29=1-\frac23=\frac13$ of $36$, i.e. $12$.
---
Q: Square $ABCD$ has side $6$, and $E$ is the midpoint of $BC$. What is the area of triangle $AED$?
L: 1
A: 18
W: 9
W: 12
W: 24
W: 36
S: Triangle $AED$ has base $AD=6$ (a side of the square). Its height is the distance from $E$ (on the opposite side $BC$) to $AD$, which is $6$.
Area $=\frac12\cdot6\cdot6=18$, exactly half the square.
---
Q: In trapezoid $ABCD$ with $AB\parallel CD$ and $AB=2CD$, the diagonals meet at $P$. If triangle $PAB$ has area $20$, what is the area of triangle $PCD$?
L: 2
A: 5
W: 10
W: 40
W: 8
W: 4
S: Since $AB\parallel CD$, triangles $PAB$ and $PCD$ are similar (alternate interior angles are equal), with ratio $\frac{AB}{CD}=2$.
Areas of similar figures scale with the square of the ratio: $\frac{[PAB]}{[PCD]}=4$.
$[PCD]=\frac{20}4=5$.
---
Q: In triangle $ABC$, $D$ is on $BC$ with $BD:DC=3:2$ and $E$ is on $AB$ with $AE:EB=1:2$. Cevians $AD$ and $CE$ meet at $P$. What is $\dfrac{CP}{PE}$?
L: 3
A: 2
W: 3
W: \frac{3}{2}
W: \frac{5}{2}
W: 4
S: Mass points. On $BC$: $BD:DC=3:2$ means $m_B\cdot3=m_C\cdot2$; take $m_B=2$, $m_C=3$.
On $AB$: $AE:EB=1:2$ means $m_A\cdot1=m_B\cdot2=4$, so $m_A=4$, and $m_E=m_A+m_B=6$.
On cevian $CE$: $\frac{CP}{PE}=\frac{m_E}{m_C}=\frac63=2$.
