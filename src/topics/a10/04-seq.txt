<h2>Sequences, Series &amp; Patterns</h2>

<h3>1. Arithmetic sequences</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Facts</h4><ul>
<li>$a_n=a_1+(n-1)d$, so $a_m-a_n=(m-n)d$.</li>
<li>Number of terms from $a$ to $L$ with step $d$: $\frac{L-a}{d}+1$.</li>
<li>Sum: $S_n=\dfrac{n(a_1+a_n)}{2}=$ (number of terms) $\times$ (average of first and last).</li></ul></div>
<p><b>Proof of the sum (Gauss).</b> Write the sum forwards and backwards:
$S=a_1+a_2+\dots+a_n$ and $S=a_n+a_{n-1}+\dots+a_1$. Add them term by term: each pair $a_k+a_{n+1-k}$ equals $a_1+a_n$ (going one step right adds $d$ on the first term and subtracts $d$ on the other). There are $n$ pairs, so $2S=n(a_1+a_n)$.</p>
<p><b>Special cases:</b> $1+2+\dots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}2$, and $1+3+5+\dots+(2n-1)=n^2$.</p>
<p><b>Symmetric terms.</b> Three terms of an arithmetic sequence can be written $a-d,\ a,\ a+d$. Their sum is $3a$, which often gives $a$ at once. The middle term of an odd-length arithmetic sequence is its average.</p>

<h3>2. Geometric sequences</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Facts</h4><ul>
<li>$a_n=a_1r^{n-1}$.</li>
<li>Finite sum: $S_n=a_1\dfrac{r^n-1}{r-1}$ for $r\ne1$.</li>
<li>Infinite sum, when $|r|\lt1$: $S=\dfrac{a_1}{1-r}$.</li></ul></div>
<p><b>Proof.</b> $S=a+ar+\dots+ar^{n-1}$ and $rS=ar+ar^2+\dots+ar^n$. Subtracting, everything cancels except $rS-S=ar^n-a$, so $S=a\frac{r^n-1}{r-1}$. When $|r|\lt1$, $r^n\to0$ as $n$ grows, leaving $\frac{-a}{r-1}=\frac a{1-r}$.</p>
<p><b>Repeating decimals</b> are infinite geometric series: $0.\overline{36}=\frac{36}{100}+\frac{36}{100^2}+\dots=\frac{36/100}{1-1/100}=\frac{36}{99}=\frac4{11}$.</p>
<p><b>Bouncing ball:</b> each rebound distance is traveled twice (up and down), so total distance $=$ first drop $+\ 2\times$(sum of the rebound heights).</p>

<h3>3. Telescoping</h3>
<p>If each term can be written as a difference $f(k)-f(k+1)$, the sum collapses: $\sum_{k=1}^{n}\big(f(k)-f(k+1)\big)=f(1)-f(n+1)$, because each middle $f$ appears once with $+$ and once with $-$.</p>
<ul><li>$\dfrac1{k(k+1)}=\dfrac1k-\dfrac1{k+1}$, so $\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac1{k(k+1)}=1-\frac1{n+1}$.</li>
<li>Products telescope too: $\frac21\cdot\frac32\cdot\frac43\cdots\frac{n+1}{n}=n+1$.</li></ul>

<h3>4. Recursion and cycles</h3>
<ul><li>Compute the first several terms. Many AMC sequences repeat with a short period; then $a_n$ depends only on $n$ modulo the period.</li>
<li><b>Fibonacci-type</b> recursions $a_n=a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}$ count tilings, stair climbs (1 or 2 steps) and binary strings with no two adjacent 1s, because the last piece is either a "1" or a "2".</li>
<li>Last digits of powers cycle: $2^n$ ends in $2,4,8,6,\dots$ (period 4).</li></ul>

<h3>5. Patterns in arrays and figures</h3>
<p>For a growing figure, count the first few stages, find the difference between consecutive stages, and decide whether it is constant (linear formula), growing by a constant (quadratic), or multiplying (exponential). A quadratic $an^2+bn+c$ has constant second differences equal to $2a$.</p>

Q: How many terms are in the arithmetic sequence $13, 17, 21, \dots, 401$?
L: 1
A: 98
W: 97
W: 99
W: 100
W: 388
S: The common difference is $4$. Number of terms $=\frac{\text{last}-\text{first}}{d}+1=\frac{401-13}{4}+1=97+1=98$.
(Forgetting the $+1$ is the classic fencepost error.)

L: 1
A: 2107
W: 2100
W: 2114
W: 1960
W: 2009
S: The multiples are $105, 112, \dots, 196$. Count: $\frac{196-105}{7}+1=13+1=14$.
Sum $=$ count $\times$ average of first and last $=14\cdot\frac{105+196}{2}=14\cdot150.5=2107$.

Q: Three numbers in arithmetic progression have sum $30$ and product $910$. What is the largest of the three?
L: 2
A: 13
W: 12
W: 14
W: 15
W: 17
S: Write them as $10-d,\ 10,\ 10+d$ (the sum $3\cdot10=30$ fixes the middle).
Product: $10(100-d^2)=910$, so $100-d^2=91$, $d^2=9$, $d=\pm3$.
The numbers are $7, 10, 13$; the largest is $13$.

Q: What is the value of $\dfrac1{1\cdot2}+\dfrac1{2\cdot3}+\dfrac1{3\cdot4}+\cdots+\dfrac1{49\cdot50}$?
L: 1
A: \frac{49}{50}
W: \frac{1}{50}
W: 1
W: \frac{50}{51}
W: \frac{48}{49}
S: Partial fractions: $\frac1{k(k+1)}=\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}$.
The sum telescopes: $\left(1-\frac12\right)+\left(\frac12-\frac13\right)+\dots+\left(\frac1{49}-\frac1{50}\right)=1-\frac1{50}=\frac{49}{50}$.

Q: A ball is dropped from a height of $27$ feet. Each time it hits the ground it bounces back up to $\frac23$ of the height it fell. What is the total distance it travels before coming to rest, in feet?
L: 2
A: 135
W: 81
W: 108
W: 162
W: 54
S: The first drop is $27$. The rebound heights are $18, 12, 8, \dots$, a geometric series with sum $\frac{18}{1-2/3}=54$.
Each rebound height is traveled twice (up, then down), so the total is $27+2\cdot54=135$ feet.

Q: A sequence has $a_1=3$, $a_2=7$, and $a_n=a_{n-1}-a_{n-2}$ for $n\ge3$. What is $a_{2026}$?
L: 2
A: -3
W: 3
W: 7
W: 4
W: -7
S: Compute terms: $a_3=7-3=4$, $a_4=4-7=-3$, $a_5=-3-4=-7$, $a_6=-7-(-3)=-4$, $a_7=-4-(-7)=3$, $a_8=3-(-4)=7$.
The pair $(3,7)$ came back, so the sequence repeats with period $6$: $3,7,4,-3,-7,-4,\dots$
$2026=6\cdot337+4$, so $a_{2026}=a_4=-3$.

Q: The repeating decimal $0.\overline{27}$ equals a fraction $\frac mn$ in lowest terms. What is $m+n$?

A: 14
W: 126
W: 27
W: 38
W: 12
S: A two-digit repeating block over $99$: $0.\overline{27}=\frac{27}{99}=\frac{3}{11}$.
So $m+n=3+11=14$. (Forgetting to reduce gives $126$.)

Q: In how many ways can you climb a staircase of $8$ steps, taking $1$ or $2$ steps at a time?
L: 2
A: 34
W: 21
W: 55
W: 28
W: 64
S: Let $w_n$ be the number of ways for $n$ steps. The last move is a $1$-step (leaving $n-1$) or a $2$-step (leaving $n-2$), so $w_n=w_{n-1}+w_{n-2}$, with $w_1=1$ and $w_2=2$.
$w_3=3, w_4=5, w_5=8, w_6=13, w_7=21, w_8=34$.

Q: What is the value of $\left(1-\dfrac12\right)\left(1-\dfrac13\right)\left(1-\dfrac14\right)\cdots\left(1-\dfrac1{100}\right)$?

Q: Figures are built from unit squares: figure $1$ has $1$ square, figure $2$ has $5$, figure $3$ has $13$, and figure $4$ has $25$. If the pattern continues, how many squares are in figure $10$?
L: 2
A: 181
W: 145
W: 200
W: 221
W: 100
S: Differences: $4, 8, 12$ grow by $4$ each time, so the count is quadratic with leading coefficient $\frac42=2$.
Try $2n^2-2n+1$: $n=1\to1$, $n=2\to5$, $n=3\to13$, $n=4\to25$ ✓. (It is $n^2+(n-1)^2$.)
Figure $10$: $100+81=181$.
