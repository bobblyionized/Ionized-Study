<h2>Polygons, Area &amp; Coordinates</h2>

<h3>1. Angles of polygons</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Theorems</h4><ul>
<li>The interior angles of an $n$-gon add to $180^\circ(n-2)$.</li>
<li>The exterior angles (one at each vertex) of any convex polygon add to $360^\circ$.</li>
<li>An $n$-gon has $\dfrac{n(n-3)}2$ diagonals.</li></ul></div>
<p><b>Proofs.</b> Drawing all diagonals from one vertex cuts a convex $n$-gon into $n-2$ triangles, whose angles make up exactly the polygon's angles. Walking once around the polygon, you turn by each exterior angle and end facing the original direction: one full turn, $360^\circ$. Each vertex connects by a diagonal to the $n-3$ vertices that are neither itself nor a neighbor, and each diagonal is counted from both ends.</p>
<p>Each interior angle of a regular $n$-gon is $180^\circ-\frac{360^\circ}{n}$: $108^\circ$ for a pentagon, $120^\circ$ for a hexagon, $135^\circ$ for an octagon.</p>

<h3>2. Areas of quadrilaterals</h3>
<ul><li><b>Parallelogram:</b> base $\times$ height (cut off a triangle from one end and move it to the other to make a rectangle).</li>
<li><b>Trapezoid:</b> $\frac{b_1+b_2}{2}h$. Two copies, one rotated, fit together into a parallelogram with base $b_1+b_2$.</li>
<li><b>Rhombus or kite</b> (perpendicular diagonals): $\frac{d_1d_2}2$. The diagonals split it into four right triangles. A rhombus's diagonals bisect each other at right angles, so its side is $\sqrt{(d_1/2)^2+(d_2/2)^2}$.</li>
<li><b>Regular hexagon</b> of side $s$: six equilateral triangles, area $\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}s^2$.</li>
<li><b>Subtraction:</b> often the easiest area is a big shape minus pieces, like a rectangle minus corner triangles.</li></ul>

<h3>3. The coordinate plane</h3>
<ul><li><b>Distance:</b> $\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}$, which is the Pythagorean theorem on the horizontal and vertical legs.</li>
<li><b>Midpoint:</b> $\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}2,\frac{y_1+y_2}2\right)$.</li>
<li><b>Slope</b> $m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}$; line $y=mx+b$. Parallel lines have equal slopes. Perpendicular lines have slopes with product $-1$. (Rotating a line by $90^\circ$ turns a run-rise step $(1,m)$ into $(-m,1)$, whose slope is $-\frac1m$.)</li>
<li><b>Distance from a point to a line</b> $ax+by+c=0$: $\dfrac{|ax_0+by_0+c|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}}$.</li></ul>

<h3>4. Shoelace formula</h3>
<div class="box"><h4>Theorem</h4>For a polygon with vertices $(x_1,y_1),\dots,(x_n,y_n)$ listed in order around the boundary,
$$\text{Area}=\tfrac12\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_iy_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i)\right|,$$ with $(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})=(x_1,y_1)$.</div>
<p><b>Why it works.</b> The term $\frac12(x_iy_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i)$ is the signed area of the triangle formed by the origin and edge $i$. Going around the polygon, these signed triangles add up to the polygon's area: the parts outside the polygon are counted once positively and once negatively, so they cancel.</p>

<h3>5. Lattice points</h3>
<ul><li>The segment from $(0,0)$ to $(a,b)$ contains $\gcd(a,b)+1$ lattice points, endpoints included. The lattice points are evenly spaced, at steps of $\left(\frac a g,\frac bg\right)$ with $g=\gcd(a,b)$.</li>
<li><b>Pick's theorem:</b> a polygon whose vertices are lattice points, with $I$ lattice points inside and $B$ on the boundary, has area $I+\frac B2-1$.</li></ul>

<h3>6. Reflections and shortest paths</h3>
<p>Reflecting over $y=x$ swaps coordinates; over the $x$-axis it negates $y$. <b>Shortest path that touches a line:</b> to go from $A$ to the line and then to $B$ (both on the same side), reflect $B$ across the line. Any path $A\to P\to B$ has the same length as $A\to P\to B'$, which is shortest when it's a straight line.</p>

Q: What is the area of the quadrilateral with vertices $(1,1)$, $(5,2)$, $(4,6)$, and $(0,4)$, in that order?
L: 2
A: \frac{31}2
W: 15
W: 16
W: 14
W: \frac{33}2
S: Shoelace: list the vertices in order and repeat the first.
$\sum x_iy_{i+1}=1\cdot2+5\cdot6+4\cdot4+0\cdot1=2+30+16+0=48$.
$\sum y_ix_{i+1}=1\cdot5+2\cdot4+6\cdot0+4\cdot1=5+8+0+4=17$.
Area $=\frac12|48-17|=\frac{31}2$.
