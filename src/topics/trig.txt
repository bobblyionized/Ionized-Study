<script type="text/plain" id="L-trig-unit">
<h2>Unit Circle &amp; Special Angles</h2>
<table class="t"><tr><th>$\theta$</th><th>$0^\circ$</th><th>$30^\circ$</th><th>$45^\circ$</th><th>$60^\circ$</th><th>$90^\circ$</th></tr>
<tr><td>$\sin$</td><td>$0$</td><td>$\frac12$</td><td>$\frac{\sqrt2}2$</td><td>$\frac{\sqrt3}2$</td><td>$1$</td></tr>
<tr><td>$\cos$</td><td>$1$</td><td>$\frac{\sqrt3}2$</td><td>$\frac{\sqrt2}2$</td><td>$\frac12$</td><td>$0$</td></tr>
<tr><td>$\tan$</td><td>$0$</td><td>$\frac{\sqrt3}3$</td><td>$1$</td><td>$\sqrt3$</td><td>undefined</td></tr></table>
<div class="box"><h4>Using the unit circle</h4><ul>
<li>Point at angle $\theta$: $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$. Radians: $\pi=180^\circ$.</li>
<li><b>Reference angle + sign:</b> All Students Take Calculus. QI: all positive; QII: sin positive; QIII: tan positive; QIV: cos positive.</li>
<li>Periods: $\sin$ and $\cos$ repeat every $360^\circ$; $\tan$ every $180^\circ$.</li>
<li>Cofunctions: $\sin(90^\circ-x)=\cos x$, so $\sin^2x+\sin^2(90^\circ-x)=1$.</li>
</ul></div>
<p>Given one ratio and the quadrant, draw a reference right triangle (often a Pythagorean triple) and attach the signs.</p>
<p>A product to remember: $\cos20^\circ\cos40^\circ\cos80^\circ=\frac18$ (multiply by $\sin20^\circ$ and apply the double-angle formula three times).</p>
</script>
<script type="text/plain" id="L-trig-id">
<h2>Identities &amp; Sum Formulas</h2>
<div class="box"><h4>Identities</h4><ul>
<li>$\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1$, $\quad1+\tan^2x=\sec^2x$.</li>
<li>$\sin2x=2\sin x\cos x$, $\quad\cos2x=\cos^2x-\sin^2x=1-2\sin^2x=2\cos^2x-1$.</li>
<li>$\sin(a\pm b)=\sin a\cos b\pm\cos a\sin b$, $\quad\cos(a\pm b)=\cos a\cos b\mp\sin a\sin b$.</li>
<li>$\tan(a\pm b)=\dfrac{\tan a\pm\tan b}{1\mp\tan a\tan b}$.</li>
<li>In terms of $t=\tan x$: $\sin2x=\frac{2t}{1+t^2}$, $\cos2x=\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}$.</li>
<li>Product to sum: $\sin a\cos b=\frac12[\sin(a+b)+\sin(a-b)]$.</li>
</ul></div>
<h3>The squaring trick</h3>
<p>If $\sin x+\cos x=s$, then $1+\sin2x=s^2$. Squaring connects sums to products.</p>
<p>$\cos^4x-\sin^4x=(\cos^2x-\sin^2x)(\cos^2x+\sin^2x)=\cos2x$.</p>
</script>
<script type="text/plain" id="L-trig-law">
<h2>Law of Sines &amp; Law of Cosines</h2>
<div class="box"><h4>Laws</h4><ul>
<li><b>Sines:</b> $\dfrac a{\sin A}=\dfrac b{\sin B}=\dfrac c{\sin C}=2R$ ($R$ is the circumradius).</li>
<li><b>Cosines:</b> $c^2=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C$. With $C=60^\circ$: $c^2=a^2+b^2-ab$. With $C=120^\circ$: $c^2=a^2+b^2+ab$.</li>
<li>Area $=\frac12ab\sin C$.</li>
</ul></div>
<h3>Finding angles</h3>
<p>$\cos C=\dfrac{a^2+b^2-c^2}{2ab}$. The largest angle is opposite the longest side. A negative cosine means that angle is obtuse.</p>
<p>Integer triangles with $60^\circ$ or $120^\circ$ angles: $3$-$5$-$7$ and $7$-$8$-$13$ ($120^\circ$); $5$-$7$-$8$ and $3$-$7$-$8$ ($60^\circ$).</p>
</script>
<script type="text/plain" id="L-trig-eq">
<h2>Trig Equations</h2>
<div class="box"><h4>Counting solutions</h4><ul>
<li>On one full period, $\sin x=k$ has 2 solutions for $|k|\lt 1$, one for $k=\pm1$, and none for $|k|>1$.</li>
<li>$\sin(nx)=k$ on $[0,2\pi)$: $nx$ runs over $n$ full periods, so multiply the count by $n$.</li>
<li>Quadratics in $\sin x$: factor, then solve each factor separately.</li>
</ul></div>
<h3>Extremes</h3>
<ul><li>$a\sin x+b\cos x$ ranges over $[-\sqrt{a^2+b^2},\sqrt{a^2+b^2}]$.</li>
<li>$\sin x\cos x=\frac12\sin2x\in[-\frac12,\frac12]$.</li></ul>
<h3>Graph intersections</h3>
<p>For equations like $\sin x=\frac x{10}$, sketch both graphs and count crossings period by period. The line leaves $[-1,1]$ when $|x|>10$.</p>
</script>

Q: How many solutions does $\sin3x=0$ have in $[0,2\pi)$?
L: 2
A: 6
W: 3
W: 7
W: 4
W: 5
S: $\sin\theta=0$ exactly when $\theta=k\pi$. So $3x=k\pi$, i.e. $x=\frac{k\pi}{3}$.
In $[0,2\pi)$: $k=0,1,2,3,4,5$ (at $k=6$, $x=2\pi$ is excluded). That's $6$ solutions.
(Compressing by $3$ fits three periods of $\sin$ into $[0,2\pi)$, each with $2$ zeros.)

Q: What is the maximum value of $3\sin x+4\cos x$?
L: 1
A: 5
W: 7
W: 4
W: 3
W: 12

In general, $a\sin x+b\cos x$ ranges from $-\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ to $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$.

Q: How many real solutions does $\sin x=\dfrac{x}{10}$ have?
L: 3
A: 7
W: 5
W: 6
W: 8
W: 3
S: Since $|\sin x|\le1$, solutions need $\left|\frac{x}{10}\right|\le1$, i.e. $|x|\le10$.
For $x\gt0$: the line $y=\frac x{10}$ (between $0$ and $1$) can meet $\sin x$ only where $\sin x\gt0$:
• on $(0,\pi)$: once (besides $x=0$),
• on $(2\pi,3\pi)\approx(6.28,9.42)$: twice (the arch rises above the line and comes back down),
• on $(3\pi,10]$: $\sin x\lt0$, so none. ($4\pi\approx12.6\gt10$.)
That's $3$ positive solutions. Both sides are odd functions, so there are also $3$ negative ones, plus $x=0$: $7$ in all.
