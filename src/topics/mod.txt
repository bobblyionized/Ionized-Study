<script type="text/plain" id="L-mod-basic">
<h2>Congruences &amp; Remainders</h2>
<div class="box"><h4>Rules</h4><ul>
<li>$a\equiv b\pmod n$ means $n\mid a-b$ ($a$ and $b$ have the same remainder).</li>
<li>Congruences can be added, subtracted, multiplied, and raised to powers. Replace each number by its remainder <i>before</i> computing.</li>
<li>Negative remainders help: $2026\equiv-1\pmod{2027}$ and $9\equiv-1\pmod{10}$.</li>
</ul></div>
<h3>Squares modulo small numbers</h3>
<table class="t"><tr><th>mod</th><th>possible $n^2$</th></tr>
<tr><td>3</td><td>0, 1</td></tr><tr><td>4</td><td>0, 1</td></tr><tr><td>5</td><td>0, 1, 4</td></tr><tr><td>8</td><td>0, 1, 4 (odd squares are always 1)</td></tr></table>
<p>So a sum of two squares can never be $\equiv3\pmod4$. Use these to rule out impossible equations quickly.</p>
</script>
<script type="text/plain" id="L-mod-cycle">
<h2>Units Digits &amp; Cycles</h2>
<div class="box"><h4>Units-digit cycles</h4>
<table class="t"><tr><th>Last digit</th><th>Cycle of powers</th></tr>
<tr><td>0, 1, 5, 6</td><td>constant</td></tr>
<tr><td>2</td><td>2, 4, 8, 6</td></tr><tr><td>3</td><td>3, 9, 7, 1</td></tr>
<tr><td>4</td><td>4, 6</td></tr><tr><td>7</td><td>7, 9, 3, 1</td></tr>
<tr><td>8</td><td>8, 4, 2, 6</td></tr><tr><td>9</td><td>9, 1</td></tr></table></div>
<p>Reduce the exponent mod 4 (using 4 instead of 0). Only the last digit of the base matters.</p>
<h3>Towers and sums</h3>
<ul><li>$7^{7^7}$: you need $7^7\bmod4$. Since $7\equiv-1$, it is $3$, so the units digit is $7^3\to3$.</li>
<li>Sums of powers: one full cycle of units digits sums to a multiple of 10 (for 3: $1+3+9+7=20$), so only the leftover partial cycle matters.</li>
<li>Last two digits: work mod 100, and look for a power that is $\equiv1$ (e.g. $7^4=2401$).</li></ul>
</script>
<script type="text/plain" id="L-mod-fermat">
<h2>Fermat's Little Theorem &amp; Euler</h2>
<div class="box"><h4>Theorems</h4><ul>
<li><b>Fermat:</b> if $p$ is prime and $p\nmid a$, then $a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p$.</li>
<li><b>Euler:</b> if $\gcd(a,n)=1$, then $a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv1\pmod n$, where $\varphi(n)=n\prod_{p\mid n}\left(1-\frac1p\right)$.</li>
<li><b>Wilson:</b> $(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p$ for prime $p$.</li>
</ul></div>
<h3>Reducing big exponents</h3>
<p>To find $a^N\bmod p$, reduce $N$ modulo $p-1$ (or modulo the actual order, which can be smaller: $3^5\equiv1\pmod{11}$, and $2^3\equiv1\pmod7$).</p>
<p>$\varphi$ examples: $\varphi(100)=40$, $\varphi(30)=8$, $\varphi(p)=p-1$, $\varphi(p^k)=p^k-p^{k-1}$.</p>
</script>
<script type="text/plain" id="L-mod-crt">
<h2>Chinese Remainder Theorem &amp; Inverses</h2>
<div class="box"><h4>CRT</h4>
If $\gcd(m,n)=1$, the system $x\equiv a\pmod m$, $x\equiv b\pmod n$ has exactly one solution modulo $mn$. For small numbers, list one sequence ($a, a+m, a+2m,\dots$) and check the other condition.</div>
<h3>Spot the pattern</h3>
<p>"Remainder 2 when divided by 3, 4 when divided by 5, 6 when divided by 7" means $x\equiv-1$ for each, so $x\equiv-1\pmod{105}$ and $x=104$.</p>
<h3>Inverses and linear congruences</h3>
<ul><li>$a^{-1}\pmod m$ exists exactly when $\gcd(a,m)=1$: find $x$ with $ax\equiv1$. For small $m$, try values.</li>
<li>To solve $ax\equiv b$, multiply both sides by $a^{-1}$.</li>
<li>Counting solutions up to $N$: the solutions form an arithmetic sequence with difference $\operatorname{lcm}$ of the moduli.</li></ul>
</script>
